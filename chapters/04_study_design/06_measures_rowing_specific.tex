\section{Rowing-Specific and Experience Measures}
\label{sec:study_design_rowing_specific_measures}

The established embodiment items are complemented by custom rowing-specific items.
These items are necessary because the study does not compare embodiment in an abstract avatar task.
It compares two ways of representing the same real ergometer action: one that preserves closer correspondence with the physical machine, and one that restores a more recognizable boat-and-oar depiction of rowing on water.
The VEQ can measure participant-reported ownership and agency, but it does not directly measure whether the real handle, the virtual hands, the virtual oars, and the rowing depiction make sense as one rowing action.
\sourcechecknote{This paragraph explains why custom rowing-specific items are needed in addition to VEQ.
Roth and Latoschik provides the primary embodiment questionnaire basis, but VEQ does not measure the specific rowing-interface questions created by this thesis: whether the real ergometer handle fits the virtual hands or oars, whether the visible hand/oar path makes sense, and whether the representation appears believable as rowing on water.
The custom items are therefore thesis-specific exploratory measures, not new validated embodiment subscales.
This separation is important because a participant can report high agency or ownership while still noticing a rowing-specific mismatch, or can prefer a boat-like scene without that preference proving stronger embodiment.}

The custom item set targets the concrete dimensions on which the two representation modes differ.
The first dimension is connection between the participant's real ergometer movement and the virtual rowing body.
This asks whether the virtual rowing body appears tied to the action the participant is actually performing.
The second dimension is the real handle as a physical, haptic, and proprioceptive anchor.
This asks whether the handle the participant holds fits with the virtual hands, handle, or oars that are seen in the headset.
The third dimension is virtual hand and oar mapping.
This asks whether the visible hand, handle, or oar path makes sense in relation to the real handle movement.
The fourth dimension is sport-action plausibility.
This asks whether the representation is believable as rowing on water.
The fifth dimension is perceived mismatch.
This asks whether participants notice conflict between the felt physical handle movement and the visible virtual movement.

The rowing-specific item categories are grounded in the rowing mechanics and VR action problem introduced earlier in the thesis.
Biomechanics and rowing-simulator work show that ergometer rowing and on-water rowing are related but not identical, especially where upper-limb, oar, blade, and water interaction are involved \parencite{lamb1989kinematicComparison,soperHume2004rowingTechnique,rauterEtAl2013rowingTransfer}.
This makes the handle/oar relation a meaningful questionnaire target rather than a cosmetic detail.
If the boat-centered mode visually restores oars and on-water rowing logic, participants may judge the representation as more rowing-like while still noticing that the real handle and virtual oar action do not fully coincide.
\sourcechecknote{This citation cluster grounds the custom rowing-specific items in rowing mechanics rather than in generic avatar embodiment.
Lamb is used because it directly compares ergometer and on-water rowing kinematics and shows that the two are related but not identical, especially around arm and oar-related movement near catch and finish.
Soper and Hume is used because it frames ergometers as legitimate rowing training/testing devices while also noting that common ergometers do not fully reproduce on-water trunk and upper-limb patterns.
Rauter et al. is used because it makes the boat-and-oar interaction explicit: realistic rowing involves rower, boat, oar, blade, water, oar handling, and haptic interaction, while ordinary ergometers simplify this into cable-driven resistance and omit oar handling or oar height in water.
Together, these sources justify asking participants about handle fit, hand/oar mapping, sport-action plausibility, and mismatch.
They do not prove that either representation will score higher; they justify why these custom items are relevant to this rowing task.}

The custom rowing-specific items are exploratory.
They are not combined into the VEQ Ownership/Acceptance or Control/Agency scores.
They are also not treated as a validated rowing embodiment scale.
Instead, they are reported as thesis-specific experience measures that help interpret why a condition may receive higher or lower ownership, agency, realism, mismatch, or preference ratings.
This makes it possible to report divergent profiles rather than forcing all responses into one overall embodiment score.

Comfort and safety symptoms are treated separately from the embodiment and rowing-specific measures.
The study includes short checks for dizziness, nausea, pain, unsafe discomfort, distress, and headset or ergometer fit issues.
These checks are included to protect participants and to interpret feasibility limitations.
They are not analyzed as ownership, agency, or sport-action plausibility scores.
A full cybersickness questionnaire is not included in the lightweight first evaluation unless the final protocol is changed before data collection.

Preference is assessed after both conditions have been completed.
These questions ask participants to compare the two representation modes directly.
Preference is a design-facing outcome rather than proof of ownership or agency.
A participant may prefer the boat-centered mode because it looks more like rowing on water, even if the erg-centered mode feels more directly connected to the real ergometer movement.
Conversely, a participant may prefer the erg-centered mode because it feels more bodily grounded, even if the boat-centered mode looks more sport-like.
For this reason, preference is reported separately and interpreted alongside the construct-specific measures.
