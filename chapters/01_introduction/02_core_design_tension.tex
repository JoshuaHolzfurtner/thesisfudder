\section{The Core Design Tension}
\label{sec:introduction_core_design_tension}

A rowing ergometer and a rowing boat do not provide the same movement interface.
On an ergometer, the user sits on a sliding seat and pulls a handle through constrained handle movement.
In a rowing boat, the rower interacts with oars that rotate around oarlocks, blades that interact with water, and a boat that moves relative to the environment.
Even when the training movement is related, the physical apparatus and spatial logic are different \parencite{lamb1989kinematicComparison,soperHume2004rowingTechnique,rauterEtAl2013rowingTransfer}%
\sourcechecknote{This paragraph grounds the design tension in rowing mechanics rather than in VR theory.
Lamb directly compares ergometer and on-water rowing and supports the nuanced premise that the movements are related but not identical: leg and trunk patterns are broadly similar in many drive-phase variables, while upper arm and forearm patterns differ, especially around catch and finish where on-water rowing involves oar-water mechanics such as lifting and feathering that are absent from ergometer rowing.
Soper and Hume support the status of the ergometer as a normal rowing training/testing device while also noting that common ergometers reproduce only parts of on-water body action and do not reproduce trunk and upper-limb patterns particularly well because of the central pulley system.
Rauter et al. support the boat-side interface: rowing involves continuous rower-boat-oar-water interaction, ordinary ergometers replace this with cable-driven resistance, and realistic rowing includes oar handling, blade-water interaction, oar angles, and boat propulsion.
The thesis inference is that these source-backed differences amount to different movement interfaces and spatial action logics.
These sources do not support VR embodiment outcomes or any claim that the ergometer is invalid as training equipment.}

A VR rowing system can respond to this difference in at least two plausible ways.
This thesis refers to these strategies as the \emph{erg-centered} and \emph{boat-centered} modes.
In the erg-centered mode, the virtual body, handle, and rowing apparatus are aligned primarily with the user's real machine and movement.
The visible hands and handle can remain close to where the user feels them, and the avatar can be organized around the physical ergometer interaction rather than around virtual oar geometry.
This preserves the physical logic of the user's current interaction and prioritizes visuomotor correspondence between the performed rowing movement and the movement shown in VR \parencite{kilteniEtAl2012senseEmbodiment}%
\sourcechecknote{This paragraph defines the thesis' erg-centered mode rather than importing a named category from the literature.
The term is thesis shorthand for a representation strategy that keeps the virtual body, handle, apparatus, and avatar movement organized around the user's real ergometer interaction.
Kilteni et al. are cited only for the theoretical relevance of visuomotor correspondence: their sense-of-embodiment framework links agency to comparisons between predicted and actual sensory consequences and to synchronous visuomotor correlations under active movement.
This source does not define the erg-centered mode, does not study rowing, and does not prove that this mode will produce stronger embodiment in the planned study.
The implementation-specific details of how handle, seat, and body state are tracked or inferred belong in Chapter~\ref{chap:system_prototype}.}

In the boat-centered mode, the system instead prioritizes the visual and sport-specific logic of rowing on water.
The user may appear to sit in a boat, move through a river, and row with oars.
This can make the virtual scene more recognizable as rowing, but it also means that the real ergometer handle movement must be interpreted as a virtual boat-and-oar action.
The resulting virtual oar movement may therefore remain responsive to the user's stroke while no longer coinciding fully with the felt position and movement of the physical handle \parencite{neumannEtAl2018interactiveVrSport,rauterEtAl2013rowingTransfer,lamb1989kinematicComparison,harrisEtAl2019visionForAction}%
\sourcechecknote{This paragraph defines the thesis' boat-centered mode rather than importing a named category from the literature.
Neumann et al. support the general VR sport mapping premise: physical effort on an exercise interface can be translated into virtual sport performance, and the review gives rowing examples where ergometer handle pulls can be represented as virtual oar movements and greater exertion can drive faster virtual movement through water and scenery.
Rauter et al. support the rowing-specific boat-and-oar side of the paragraph: realistic rowing involves rower, boat, oar, blade, oarlock, and water interaction, including oar handling and blade-water mechanics that ordinary ergometers do not reproduce.
Lamb supports the mechanical mismatch by showing that ergometer and on-water rowing are related but differ in upper-limb and oar-related movement around catch and finish.
Harris is used as a general VR action caution: visually convincing VR does not automatically preserve the sensory and haptic conditions of real-world action.
Together, these sources justify the paragraph's thesis inference that a boat-centered representation can be visually recognizable as rowing while still transforming the felt ergometer handle movement into a virtual oar action.
They do not prove that the boat-centered mode will be preferred or embodied more strongly.}

The comparison is therefore not between a real and an artificial representation of rowing.
Both modes are representations of an indoor rowing action under different constraints.
The erg-centered mode preserves the physical logic of the user's actual device and movement, but can still place that indoor action inside a mediated rowing environment.
The boat-centered mode preserves the recognizable boat-and-oar logic of the sport, but must transform the real ergometer interaction into a virtual oar action.
There is no neutral third option in which the indoor ergometer simply becomes on-water rowing without remainder \parencite{lamb1989kinematicComparison,rauterEtAl2013rowingTransfer,neumannEtAl2018interactiveVrSport,harrisEtAl2019visionForAction}%
\sourcechecknote{This paragraph is a conceptual guardrail.
It prevents the comparison from being read as real rowing versus fake rowing or correct rowing versus wrong rowing.
Lamb and Rauter support the mechanical premise that ergometer rowing and on-water rowing are related but not interchangeable: Lamb documents upper-limb and oar-related differences between ergometer and on-water rowing, while Rauter emphasizes the rower-boat-oar-water interaction that ordinary ergometers simplify.
Neumann supports the VR sport premise that physical effort on an interface can be translated into virtual sport action, including rowing examples where handle pulls become virtual oar movements.
Harris supports the caution that visually convincing VR action does not automatically reproduce the sensory and haptic conditions of real-world action.
The thesis inference is that both modes are compromises: the erg-centered mode preserves the user's actual physical interaction but still mediates it in VR, while the boat-centered mode preserves the recognizable boat-and-oar form of the sport but must transform the physical ergometer interaction.
The sources support the premises for this framing, but the statement that there is no neutral third option is thesis synthesis.}

This makes VR indoor rowing a useful case for studying a broader XR design problem. Many XR sport and exercise systems use stationary or simplified physical equipment to represent more complex activities. The system can either make the virtual action correspond closely to the user's real movement, or it can transform the movement to better match the represented activity. Both choices solve one part of the problem while introducing another.

In this thesis, this design problem is framed as a trade-off between visuomotor congruence and realistic sport depiction. Visuomotor congruence refers to the correspondence between the user's performed movement and the movement they see in the virtual environment. Realistic sport depiction refers to the extent to which the virtual scene and action resemble the sport being represented. The two can support each other, but in indoor rowing they can also come apart.
