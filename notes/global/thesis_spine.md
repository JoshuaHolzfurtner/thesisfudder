# Thesis Spine

## One-sentence thesis

This thesis develops and evaluates a virtual reality (VR) rowing system that compares an **ergometer‐congruent representation**—which prioritizes precise visuomotor alignment between real and virtual movements—with a **boat‐realistic representation**—which prioritizes sport‐specific depiction even when real and virtual handle motions diverge. The central question is whether embodiment in XR rowing is better supported by precise movement correspondence or by a more realistic rowing representation.

## Primary contribution

An end‑to‑end XR rowing mechanism and prototype that supports two representation modes with different levels of visuomotor congruence and realism. This includes the hardware/software implementation and the mapping strategies that align (or intentionally misalign) the user’s real handle motion with the avatar’s motion.

## Empirical contribution

A user study comparing the two modes on measures of **sense of ownership (SoO)**, **sense of agency (SoA)**, and broader **sense of embodiment (SoE)**, with optional presence/realism ratings and physiological responses. The study design, data collection, and analysis constitute a concrete evaluation of how spatial alignment, temporal congruence, and avatar realism influence embodied experience.

## Interpretive contribution

An analysis and discussion of how the trade‑off between machine incorporation and sport realism (a version of the **cyborg’s dilemma**) shapes immersion, perceived realism, and the feeling that the virtual rowing body and stroke are one’s own. The findings are situated within embodiment theory and VR sports research to derive design guidelines for future XR sport and training systems.

## What this thesis is not

- It is **not** a general theory of embodiment or a proof of neurocognitive causality.
- It is **not** a neuroscience or psychological study that establishes causal mechanisms.
- It is **not** a commercial product evaluation or market survey of rowing systems.
- It does **not** claim that one representation is universally superior for all users or contexts; instead, it explores design trade‑offs.
