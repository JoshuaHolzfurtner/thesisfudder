# Thesis notes — Di Pino et al. 2014, “Augmentation-related brain plasticity”

## Citation identity

Di Pino, G., Maravita, A., Zollo, L., Guglielmelli, E., & Di Lazzaro, V. (2014). *Augmentation-related brain plasticity*. Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience, 8, Article 109. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2014.00109

## What this paper is

This is a neuroscience review about human augmentation and brain plasticity. It reviews how the brain adapts to tools, artificial sensors, external effectors, prostheses, sensory substitution, neuroprostheses, supernumerary limbs, and brain-machine interfaces.

It is not an XR rowing paper, not a rowing biomechanics paper, and not a direct avatar-embodiment experiment. Its thesis value is conceptual: it gives a cautious background for the idea that body representation, peripersonal space, and tool-mediated action are plastic rather than fixed.

For the XR rowing thesis, this should be treated as an optional / yellow-tag support source for tool-use and augmentation-related plasticity. It can help explain why a virtual oar system is not automatically absurd just because the visible oar/arm motion differs from the real erg handle. However, it must not be used as proof that users will embody offset virtual oars.

## Content summary

Di Pino et al. argue that modern human augmentation requires reconsidering tool use and human-machine interaction. Traditional tools extend human action, but modern devices such as robotic prostheses, artificial sensors, brain-machine interfaces, and supernumerary effectors may become more tightly integrated with the nervous system.

The review frames brain plasticity as the ability of the brain to adapt its structural and functional connectivity in response to new functions, new ways of performing old functions, or the loss/suppression of sensorimotor abilities. The authors discuss multiple related paradigms:

- sensorimotor training-induced plasticity,
- cognitive enhancement,
- cross-modal plasticity and sensory substitution,
- tool-use-induced plasticity,
- artifact embodiment,
- prosthesis and motor-output replacement,
- augmentation-induced plasticity.

The paper’s most relevant idea is that human brain/body representation is not rigid. Repeated tool use can alter body schema and peripersonal space. Artificial or prosthetic devices can also be integrated into action and body representations when the mapping is sufficiently learnable, goal-directed, body-like, and supported by congruent sensory feedback.

The review also discusses rubber hand illusion, mirror-box illusion, supernumerary limbs, robotic hand training, prosthesis embodiment, and the role of frontoparietal, premotor, parietal, somatosensory, cerebellar, insular, and mirror-system networks in tool control and artificial effector integration.

A central caution is that plasticity can support integration, but it can also produce unnatural or maladaptive changes. The authors do not claim that any artificial device automatically becomes embodied. Instead, embodiment and tool integration depend on factors such as anthropomorphism, anatomical plausibility, active control, synchronous movement, veridical sensory feedback, and repeated training.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

This paper is relevant because the boat-centered XR rowing mode asks the user to physically pull a central ergometer handle while visually seeing a rowing body use oars. This creates a controlled mismatch:

- the real handle follows an ergometer trajectory,
- the virtual oars and oar handles follow a more boat-like / sport-action trajectory,
- the avatar IK may rely partly on real inputs and partly on prerecorded or procedural rowing animation,
- at points such as finish, release, feathering, and recovery, the rhythm of on-water rowing may differ from the rhythm of the real erg handle.

Di Pino et al. help frame this as a tool-mediated action problem rather than simply as “wrong hands.” Oars can be understood as tools or external effectors that extend action into the environment. Tool-use research suggests that body schema and peripersonal space can adapt to tools and artificial devices under the right conditions.

However, this is only a plausibility argument. It does not prove that users will embody virtual oars or tolerate large offsets. It mainly supports a careful sentence such as:

- Humans can adapt to tool-mediated action mappings, so a virtual-oar representation may be acceptable if the temporal rhythm, action outcome, and rowing logic remain coherent.
- But the offset oar IK should still be evaluated, because tool-use plasticity does not guarantee ownership, agency, or congruence.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- Human augmentation involves artificial sensors/effectors that extend or modify human sensorimotor capabilities.
- Brain plasticity allows sensorimotor representations to adapt to new functions, new tools, or new input/output mappings.
- Sensorimotor training can change motor and sensory cortical representations and can reduce attentional load as proficiency increases.
- Tool use recruits premotor and parietal networks and involves perceptual, conceptual, and motor representations of the tool.
- Repeated tool use can alter peripersonal space and body schema.
- Classic rake-use findings suggest that visual receptive fields can extend along or around a used tool.
- Rubber hand illusion and related paradigms show that ownership can be shifted toward artificial or virtual limbs under congruent multisensory conditions.
- Supernumerary artificial or virtual limbs can sometimes be incorporated into body representation, but ownership and action-control integration may dissociate.
- Anthropomorphism, anatomical plausibility, active synchronous movement, and realistic sensory feedback facilitate embodiment of artificial limbs.
- Sensory feedback that is veridical and close to normal physiology is more likely to support artifact embodiment.
- Prosthesis and neuroprosthesis training can reshape body representation and sensorimotor cortical organization.
- Plasticity is not automatically positive; mismatches between mental body image and physical body can potentially create maladaptive or uncomfortable effects.
- The paper’s broad conclusion is that anthropomorphic sensorized devices can provide sensory afferences that support tool embodiment, body-representation reshaping, and changes in the sense of self.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### Tool embodiment / oars as tools

Highly relevant as an optional conceptual bridge. In boat rowing, the oar is not simply an object in the scene. It is a tool through which the rower applies force and produces boat motion. In XR rowing, virtual oars may function as visually extended tools even when the user physically holds a central erg handle.

This source can support a cautious statement that virtual oars may be better understood as tool-mediated action representations than as literal direct hand replicas.

### Body schema plasticity

Relevant. The paper supports the idea that body schema and peripersonal space are plastic and can adapt through repeated tool use. This is useful for discussing whether offset or extended rowing tools might become acceptable after practice.

### Peripersonal space

Relevant. Tool use can extend the actionable space around the body. For rowing, the oar extends the effect of the user’s movement beyond the hand/handle toward the blade and water. This may be useful for future discussion of virtual oars and action-outcome coupling.

### Offset oar-arm IK

Relevant but only cautiously. This paper supports the possibility that users can adapt to non-trivial tool-mediated mappings. It does not prove that offset virtual oar arms will feel natural, owned, or embodied.

The finish/release region may be especially important because the real erg handle rhythm and boat/oar release rhythm can diverge. Any mismatch in timing, force, or visible hand/oar behavior could reduce agency or congruence. This paper helps frame that issue as an empirical design question.

### Sense of embodiment

Indirect relevance. The paper discusses embodiment of tools, rubber hands, prostheses, and artificial limbs, but it is not the main source for XR avatar embodiment. Use Kilteni et al., Gonzalez-Franco and Peck, Mottelson et al., and Girondini et al. for direct embodiment framing.

### Sense of agency

Indirect relevance. Tool control and artificial-effector control involve learning mappings between intention, movement, device behavior, and action outcome. This is relevant to agency in XR rowing, especially if the virtual oar motion is not one-to-one with the real handle.

### Visuomotor congruence

Relevant. Active synchronous movement and congruent sensorimotor feedback support embodiment of artificial limbs/tools. In XR rowing, temporal congruence may matter even if spatial trajectories differ.

### Haptic / force congruence

Relevant. The paper emphasizes the importance of sensory feedback for embodiment. This supports the idea that the real erg handle and resistance remain powerful physical anchors, while virtual oars are visually and functionally plausible but not fully force-congruent.

### Sport-action congruence

Indirectly relevant. The boat-centered mode may gain sport-action congruence by showing oars and boat behavior, but this is not the same as direct body congruence. Tool-use plasticity may help users tolerate or learn this mapping, but it does not remove the tradeoff.

### Cyborg’s dilemma / augmentation

Relevant conceptually. The paper fits the broader idea that human-machine systems can reshape body representation. If the thesis mentions Biocca-style embodiment or adaptation to technical bodies, Di Pino et al. can support the neuroscience side of that discussion. Use sparingly.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

The erg-centered mode prioritizes real-machine congruence. The user holds a real handle and experiences real resistance, seat movement, and body posture. Di Pino et al. support the broader idea that sensorimotor feedback and congruent action mappings matter for body/tool integration.

In this mode:
- the real handle is the main tool,
- the virtual body can closely follow the real action,
- haptic/proprioceptive feedback is strong,
- agency may be easier to support because the virtual movement maps tightly to the physical movement.

### Boat-centered mode

The boat-centered mode visually represents a more sport-faithful rowing action with oars, oarlocks, blades, catch, finish, release, and recovery. Di Pino et al. are useful because the oar can be framed as a tool or artificial extension of action.

In this mode:
- the virtual oar may function as an extended tool,
- users may adapt to the mapping if the rhythm, visual outcome, and action logic are coherent,
- however, the real handle trajectory and virtual oar-handle trajectory may diverge,
- the mapping should be treated as an approximation and not assumed to produce ownership or full embodiment.

### Core tradeoff

This paper helps nuance the core tradeoff:

- Erg-centered mode: stronger bodily/device congruence.
- Boat-centered mode: stronger sport-action/tool-action congruence.
- Tool-use plasticity suggests that humans may adapt to tool-mediated mappings, but embodiment is conditional and should be evaluated.

This supports a cautious framing:
- The boat-centered mode is not invalid simply because it departs from a literal one-to-one erg mapping.
- But offset oar IK remains a design risk because body ownership, agency, and action-control congruence may weaken when mappings become too indirect.

## What not to overclaim

- Do not claim this paper proves virtual oars become embodied.
- Do not claim this paper proves offset oar-arm IK will be accepted.
- Do not claim tool-use plasticity equals body ownership.
- Do not claim body schema plasticity means any mismatch is fine.
- Do not use this paper as direct evidence for XR rowing.
- Do not use this as the main source for avatar embodiment.
- Do not claim a short thesis exposure can produce long-term brain plasticity unless specifically measured.
- Do not claim that users will adapt during one short evaluation session.
- Do not claim that virtual oars are equivalent to prostheses or supernumerary limbs; they are better framed as tool-mediated visual/action representations.
- Do not make this a major neuroscience tangent unless the thesis explicitly discusses augmentation or tool embodiment.

Tool-use and augmentation research suggests that the body schema is not rigid, but can adapt to artificial or tool-mediated action. ## Candidate glossary wording

### Tool-mediated action

Tool-mediated action describes action performed through an external object or device rather than directly through the body alone. In rowing, the oar is a tool that extends the rower’s action from the hands to the blade and water. In XR rowing, a virtual oar can therefore be treated as a tool-mediated action representation. This may make some visual offset between real handle motion and virtual oar motion acceptable, but it does not guarantee embodiment or agency.

### Body schema plasticity

Body schema plasticity refers to the ability of the nervous system to update the body’s action-oriented representation through training, tool use, sensory feedback, or artificial effectors. This concept is useful for explaining why users may adapt to virtual rowing tools over time. However, short-term acceptance of an offset oar mapping should be evaluated rather than assumed.

### Virtual oars as tools

In the boat-centered mode, virtual oars should not be treated as literal body parts. They are better framed as visually extended tools that translate the user’s rowing action into boat movement. This framing allows the thesis to discuss the virtual oar mapping cautiously: the mapping may be learnable and sport-plausible, but it can still reduce bodily/device congruence compared with an erg-centered representation.
