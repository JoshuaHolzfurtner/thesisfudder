# Thesis notes — Slater 2009, “Place illusion and plausibility can lead to realistic behaviour in immersive virtual environments”

## Citation identity

Slater, M. (2009). *Place illusion and plausibility can lead to realistic behaviour in immersive virtual environments*. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences, 364(1535), 3549-3557. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0138

## What this paper is

This is a conceptual/theoretical VR paper by Mel Slater. It explains why people may respond realistically to events in immersive virtual environments even though they know the virtual events are not physically real.

It introduces and distinguishes several important concepts:

- immersion,
- sensorimotor contingencies,
- place illusion (PI),
- plausibility illusion (Psi),
- response-as-if-real (RAIR),
- the role of the virtual body as a point where place and plausibility can fuse.

For the XR rowing thesis, this is a high-priority source for the general XR/theory/glossary layer. It is not a rowing paper and not a body-ownership paper in the narrow sense, but it is very useful for separating presence/place, plausibility, and embodiment.

## Content summary

Slater argues that immersive virtual reality should not only be understood as simulation of physical reality. VR can create experiences that go beyond normal physical reality, including transformed sense of place and altered self-representation.

The paper proposes two major components that contribute to realistic response in VR:

1. Place illusion (PI):
   - the illusion of being in the virtual place,
   - often described as presence or “being there,”
   - supported by immersion and sensorimotor contingencies.

2. Plausibility illusion (Psi):
   - the illusion that the depicted events are actually occurring,
   - supported by events that respond to or concern the participant,
   - dependent on scenario credibility and participant-directed events.

When both place illusion and plausibility illusion occur, participants may respond to virtual events as if they were real. Slater calls this response-as-if-real (RAIR).

The paper defines immersion as a property of the system rather than a subjective feeling. Immersion depends on what the system allows the user to do and perceive. In particular, immersive systems support sensorimotor contingencies: when the user moves the head or body, the virtual display changes in ways that approximate ordinary perception in the real world. For example, if the user turns their head in an HMD and the visual scene updates accordingly, that supports the normal action of looking around. If the user reaches to touch a virtual object and feels nothing, that breaks or limits the system’s sensorimotor support.

Place illusion is the subjective consequence of this kind of immersive sensorimotor support. It is the illusion of being located in the virtual place, despite knowing that one is not physically there.

Plausibility illusion is different. It is about whether the events in the virtual environment seem to be actually happening. Slater emphasizes that Psi does not require perfect physical realism. A low-fidelity character or environment can still produce plausible response if the events behave in a way that relates to the participant. For example, a virtual person looking at the participant, speaking to them, reacting to their movement, or showing pain in response to the participant’s action can support plausibility.

The paper also discusses the virtual body. Seeing a virtual body where one expects one’s body to be can support place illusion. If the virtual body moves in synchrony with the user’s real body, it also supports plausibility because the external event in the virtual world directly correlates with the user’s proprioception and action. Slater links this to rubber-hand and virtual-body illusion research, where synchronous multisensory stimulation can produce ownership-like experiences.

The central claim is that immersive VR can elicit realistic behavior when the user feels located in the virtual place and when the virtual events appear to be genuinely happening in relation to them.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

This paper is useful because the XR rowing thesis should not collapse all XR experience terms into one word like “immersion.”

For the thesis, Slater’s distinction helps separate:

- **immersion**: what the system technically supports,
- **place illusion**: whether the user feels located in the virtual rowing environment,
- **plausibility illusion**: whether the rowing events feel like they are actually happening,
- **embodiment**: whether the avatar/body/hand action feels self-related,
- **congruence**: whether real and virtual sensory/action cues fit together.

This is especially useful for the two thesis modes:

1. Erg-centered / high-congruence mode:
   - may support plausibility through tight real/virtual action-response coupling,
   - the user sees a virtual body/handle that matches the real handle and body movement,
   - the virtual action directly responds to the user’s physical rowing.

2. Boat-centered / sport-depiction mode:
   - may support place and sport plausibility through boat, oars, river motion, blades, and rowing context,
   - but may reduce direct bodily/device congruence if the virtual oar/hand path differs from the real erg handle.

Slater helps phrase the core tradeoff more carefully:
- a scene can have strong place illusion without strong embodiment,
- a rowing action can be plausible as sport depiction while still being less congruent with the real device,
- a physically congruent erg-centered body can feel directly controllable even if it is less realistic as on-water rowing.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- Immersion is a property of the VR system, not the same as the subjective feeling of presence.
- Immersion can be described through the valid sensorimotor and effectual actions the system supports.
- Sensorimotor contingencies are the action-perception rules that let users perceive the virtual world through normal movements such as turning the head, moving closer, or looking around.
- Place illusion (PI) is the illusion of being in the virtual place.
- Place illusion depends on the extent to which the system supports realistic sensorimotor contingencies.
- Plausibility illusion (Psi) is the illusion that the depicted events are actually occurring.
- Psi depends on whether events are credible and whether they directly relate to the participant.
- Psi does not require perfect graphical realism; participant-directed events can still produce realistic responses.
- When PI and Psi both occur, users may show response-as-if-real (RAIR).
- The virtual body is a powerful site where PI and Psi can combine.
- Synchronous movement of a virtual body with the user’s real body can support the feeling that the virtual body is self-related.
- Place illusion, plausibility illusion, and embodiment should be distinguished rather than treated as the same construct.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### Immersion

High relevance. Slater defines immersion as a system property related to supported valid actions and sensorimotor contingencies. This is useful for avoiding vague use of “immersion” as a feeling.

### Place illusion / presence

High relevance. Place illusion can explain the user’s feeling of being in the virtual rowing environment, on the river, in the boat, or in the rowing scene. This is separate from whether the avatar feels owned.

### Plausibility illusion

Very high relevance. Plausibility illusion maps well to XR rowing because the virtual boat, oars, avatar, handle, speed, water, and world response need to behave as if they are really responding to the user.

### Response-as-if-real

Moderate-high relevance. RAIR is useful for explaining why users may react to virtual rowing, virtual impact, virtual movement, or virtual social/competitive situations even when they know the scene is simulated.

### Sense of embodiment

Relevant but not the main source. Slater touches the virtual body and rubber-hand-style ownership, but use Kilteni, Mottelson, Girondini, Kalckert/Ehrsson, and related embodiment papers for the core embodiment theory.

### Sensorimotor contingencies

Very high relevance. This is a strong term for the erg-centered mode because that mode tries to preserve action-perception rules: when the user pulls the real handle, the virtual handle/body responds in a matching way.

### Visuomotor congruence

High relevance. Slater’s discussion of the virtual body and synchronous movement supports the broader logic that synchrony between real movement and virtual movement helps make the virtual body/event plausible and self-related.

### Erg-centered mode

High relevance. The erg-centered mode may support plausibility through tight action coupling: the virtual body and handle behave in direct response to real rowing movement. This can make the interaction feel less like watching a scene and more like acting in it.

### Boat-centered mode

High relevance. The boat-centered mode may support place and sport plausibility through a convincing rowing scene. However, Slater’s framework helps warn that visual sport realism alone is not enough if the action-response relationship is not credible.

### Vection

Indirect relevance. The paper is not a vection source. Use Palmisano/Kooijman/Riecke for vection. Slater can still support the broader VR feeling-of-place framework.

### Threat / impact probe

Indirect relevance. RAIR helps explain why users may react to virtual threats or impacts. But use Ma & Hommel for the specific caution that severe threat response is not a clean ownership measure.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

The erg-centered mode can be described as emphasizing sensorimotor and action-response plausibility. The user’s real handle, seat, and body motion are mapped closely to the avatar and virtual handle. This supports a coherent loop:

- action: user pulls the handle,
- perception: virtual hands/body/handle move accordingly,
- effect: boat/world/metrics respond.

In Slater’s terms, the mode may support plausibility because events in the virtual world directly relate to the participant’s real actions.

### Boat-centered mode

The boat-centered mode can be described as emphasizing place and sport plausibility. The user sees a more rowing-faithful environment with a boat, oars, oarlocks, blades, and water response. This can make the scenario more credible as “rowing,” even if the user is still physically on an ergometer.

However, this mode risks a mismatch if the virtual oar/hand motion does not respond credibly to the real handle movement. Slater’s framework helps separate two questions:
- Does the user feel present in the rowing place?
- Do the rowing events appear to be genuinely caused by and related to the user?

### Core tradeoff

Slater helps clarify that the thesis is not simply “more realism is better.”

There are multiple realism/plausibility layers:

- place plausibility: does the user feel located in the rowing scene?
- event plausibility: do virtual events seem to happen in relation to the user?
- body plausibility: does the virtual body respond like the user’s body?
- sport plausibility: does the rowing look and behave like rowing?
- device congruence: does the virtual action match the real erg?

The erg-centered mode may be stronger in device/action plausibility. The boat-centered mode may be stronger in place/sport plausibility. The thesis comparison asks how these different forms interact with embodiment.

## What not to overclaim

- Do not use this paper as direct evidence for XR rowing embodiment.
- Do not claim place illusion equals body ownership.
- Do not claim plausibility illusion equals embodiment.
- Do not claim graphical realism alone produces realistic response.
- Do not claim users forget that VR is not real; Slater explicitly keeps the distinction that users know the events are not physically real.
- Do not treat immersion as a subjective feeling; in Slater’s framing, immersion is a property of the system.
- Do not use this as a vection definition source.
- Do not use this as a rowing-related-work source.

## Candidate thesis paragraph

Slater distinguishes immersive system properties from the subjective illusions that may arise in virtual reality. In his framework, immersion depends on the sensorimotor contingencies supported by the system, while place illusion describes the feeling of being located in the virtual place and plausibility illusion describes the feeling that the depicted events are actually occurring. This distinction is useful for XR rowing because a virtual river or boat scene may support the feeling of being in a rowing environment, while the avatar, handle, oar, and boat responses determine whether the rowing action itself appears to be happening in relation to the user. The erg-centered and boat-centered modes can therefore be understood as emphasizing different forms of plausibility: direct bodily/device action congruence in the former, and sport-specific rowing context in the latter.

## Candidate short thesis sentence

Slater’s distinction between place illusion and plausibility illusion helps separate feeling located in a virtual rowing environment from believing that the virtual rowing events are coherently happening in response to the user.

## Candidate glossary wording

### Immersion

In Slater’s framework, immersion is not the user’s feeling of being absorbed. It is a property of the VR system: the extent to which the system supports valid sensorimotor and effectual actions, such as looking around, moving closer, reaching, touching, or changing the environment. In XR rowing, immersion therefore depends not only on graphics, but also on tracking, latency, field of view, spatial audio, physical input, and how well rowing actions produce meaningful virtual responses.

### Sensorimotor contingencies

Sensorimotor contingencies are the action-perception rules that connect movement to sensory change. In VR, turning the head should change the visual scene as it would in the real world. In XR rowing, pulling the handle, moving the seat, leaning the body, and changing stroke rhythm should produce coherent virtual body, boat, and world responses.

### Place illusion

Place illusion is the illusion of being located in the virtual place. In XR rowing, this means the user may feel present in a rowing environment, such as a river, boathouse, or boat scene. Place illusion should not be confused with body ownership or rowing embodiment.

### Plausibility illusion

Plausibility illusion is the illusion that the depicted events are actually happening. In XR rowing, this concerns whether the boat, avatar, handle, oars, water, and world behave as if they are really responding to the user’s rowing. A scene can look realistic but still feel implausible if the action-response logic is wrong.

### Response-as-if-real

Response-as-if-real describes realistic behavioral, emotional, or physiological reactions to virtual events. Users may react as if a virtual event matters even while knowing it is not physically real. In XR rowing, this can apply to racing, virtual impact, threat/impact probes, or the feeling of moving through a believable rowing world.
