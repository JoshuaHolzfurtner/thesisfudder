# Thesis notes — Kocur et al. (2022), RHI in VR and real world

## Full citation candidate

Kocur, M., Kalus, A., Bogon, J., Henze, N., Wolff, C., & Schwind, V. (2022). *The Rubber Hand Illusion in Virtual Reality and the Real World - Comparable but Different*. In *Proceedings of VRST '22*. ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3562939.3565614

## Working tag

**Green-yellow / bridge source.**  
Use as a bridge between classical RHI and VR embodiment/BOI. It is especially useful for saying that RHI principles can transfer to VR, but that VR and real-world RHI are comparable rather than identical.

## Why it matters for the thesis

This paper directly supports the planned theory chain:

1. RHI is a real-world body-ownership paradigm.
2. VR embodiment research inherits much of its logic from RHI.
3. RHI-style effects can be reproduced in VR.
4. However, VR changes the situation because the user may experience a virtual body, altered depth/FOV, and potentially full-body substitution.

For the rowing thesis, this helps justify using RHI/BOI literature to frame a VR/XR rowing self-avatar experiment without pretending the two domains are exactly the same.

## Core research question of the paper

The authors ask whether the Rubber Hand Illusion produces comparable effects in the real world and in VR. They replicate the RHI with both a physical rubber hand and a virtual rubber hand in a virtual replica of the same experimental setup.

## Study design

- 24 participants.
- Within-subjects design.
- Two factors:
  - **Environment:** real world vs VR.
  - **Synchrony:** synchronous vs asynchronous stroking.
- Measures:
  - proprioceptive drift,
  - RHI questionnaire,
  - skin temperature of stimulated and unstimulated hands.
- The asynchronous condition served as the control condition.
- Condition order was counterbalanced.

## Main findings

### 1. RHI worked in both real world and VR

Synchronous stroking produced stronger RHI effects than asynchronous stroking in both environments. This appeared in both proprioceptive drift and questionnaire ratings.

### 2. VR and real-world RHI were comparable, but not identical

The RHI effect was broadly comparable across real and VR environments. Bayesian tests gave anecdotal to moderate evidence that the synchrony effect was similar across environments.

However, proprioceptive drift was generally larger in VR than in the real world. The authors caution that this should not automatically be interpreted as stronger ownership, because VR-specific factors such as HMD field of view and depth perception may influence spatial estimates.

### 3. Proprioceptive drift and questionnaire embodiment did not correlate cleanly

The authors found no significant relationship between proprioceptive drift and most RHI questionnaire dimensions. This supports the idea that proprioceptive drift and subjective ownership may reflect partly different mechanisms.

For the thesis: do not treat drift/detection tasks as the same thing as questionnaire ownership. They are complementary.

### 4. Skin temperature effects were observed, but remain hard to interpret

The study found a temperature-pattern effect: both hands warmed over time, but during synchronous stroking the stimulated and unstimulated hands diverged more. The stimulated hand warmed less relative to the unstimulated hand.

The authors do **not** conclude cleanly that ownership caused a true cooling of the hand. They explicitly acknowledge that temperature changes could reflect perceptual conflict, environmental factors, or social touch/experimental conditions.

For the thesis: skin temperature and related hard physiological measures are interesting but not safe as primary evidence.

### 5. VR may involve full-body substitution even in a hand-focused RHI task

The authors note that in VR, the virtual body may substitute the entire real body, not just the hand. This is very relevant to the rowing thesis because the prototype uses a full-body self-avatar rather than only an isolated virtual hand.

## Best thesis use

Use this paper in the theory section as a bridge:

> RHI principles have been transferred into VR, and direct comparisons suggest that RHI effects in VR and the real world can be comparable, while VR also introduces medium-specific factors such as field of view, depth perception, and whole-body substitution.

Use it to justify:

- why RHI belongs in the thesis,
- why VR embodiment can inherit RHI concepts,
- why the relationship between subjective ownership and spatial drift is not simple,
- why physiological measures should remain exploratory,
- why full-body VR avatar embodiment is not reducible to hand-only RHI.

## Possible quote targets to verify in PDF

- RHI knowledge is the basis for understanding VR body ownership and avatar embodiment.
- Effects on skin temperature caused by RHI are controversial.
- There is no direct one-to-one equivalence between real RHI and VR; VR can change drift through FOV/depth/full-body substitution.
- Proprioceptive drift and questionnaire ratings are not necessarily correlated.
- RHI can be leveraged in VR to study BOIs and avatar embodiment.

## Relevance to rowing project

### Erg-centered mode

The erg-centered mode uses a real physical handle and attempts tight visuomotor/visuoproprioceptive alignment. This resembles the RHI/BOI logic of congruent seen and felt body/action cues.

### Boat-centered mode

The boat-centered mode deliberately introduces offset/mismatch between real hand/handle path and virtual oar/hand representation. Kocur et al. helps remind us that proprioceptive drift, perceived ownership, and VR spatial perception can dissociate.

### Evaluation

If an exploratory hand-offset or drift-style task is included, this paper is useful because it shows drift can be measured in both real and VR RHI settings, but also warns that drift should not be equated directly with subjective ownership.

## Suggested thesis phrasing

> The RHI is an important precursor to VR body-ownership research, but VR does not simply reproduce the physical RHI unchanged. Kocur et al. found broadly comparable RHI effects in real and virtual environments, while also observing VR-specific differences in proprioceptive drift. This supports using RHI-derived concepts in VR embodiment research, but also motivates treating subjective ownership, spatial drift, and physiological responses as related but distinct measures.

## Priority

Read/cite selectively. This is not the main theory spine like Kilteni/Mottelson/VEQ, but it is a very useful bridge paper for the transition from real-world RHI to VR embodiment.
