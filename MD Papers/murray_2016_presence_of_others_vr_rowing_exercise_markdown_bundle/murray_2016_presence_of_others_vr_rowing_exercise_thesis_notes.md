# Thesis notes — Murray et al. 2016, “The effects of the presence of others during a rowing exercise in a virtual reality environment”

## Citation identity

Murray, E. G., Neumann, D. L., Moffitt, R. L., & Thomas, P. R. (2016). *The effects of the presence of others during a rowing exercise in a virtual reality environment*. Psychology of Sport and Exercise, 22, 328–336. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2015.09.007

## What this paper is

This is an experimental VR-rowing/exercise psychology paper. It investigates whether rowing on an ergometer in a projected virtual rowing environment improves performance and affective response, and whether adding another virtual companion/avatar further increases performance.

It is not an embodiment paper in the strict body-ownership sense, and it is not about erg-centered versus boat-centered avatar embodiment. However, it is close enough to the thesis project to be useful as related work because it uses:

- a Concept2 Model D ergometer,
- a projected virtual rowing environment,
- a visible rowing avatar from a third-person rear view,
- avatar stroke timing matched to the ergometer,
- a companion/avatar condition,
- performance and affective outcome measures.

For this thesis, it belongs in the VR/XR rowing related-work section, especially the pile about VR rowing improving motivation/performance and previous systems using projected rowing avatars.

## Content summary

The study asked whether VR can improve aerobic rowing performance and affective response, and whether the presence of another individual in the virtual environment adds a further motivational effect.

The authors compared three conditions:

1. No virtual reality (NVR):
   - participants rowed without visual display or performance feedback.

2. Individual virtual reality (IVR):
   - participants rowed in a projected VR rowing environment alone.

3. Companion virtual reality (CVR):
   - participants rowed in the same projected VR rowing environment but with a second boat/avatar representing a supposed teammate.

The study used 60 female participants aged 18–30 years, assigned to 20 participants per condition. Participants rowed for 9 minutes on a Concept2 Model D indoor rowing ergometer. The PM3 monitor was covered so participants did not see quantitative performance feedback. The ergometer was connected to Netathlon 2 XF software, which projected a virtual rowing environment onto a wall in front of the ergometer.

In the VR conditions, participants viewed the Head of the Charles course with scenery such as river bends, trees, bridges, and buildings. The visual display showed a third-person rowing avatar from behind. The avatar’s stroke timing matched the participant’s rowing strokes on the ergometer. In the companion condition, a second boat/avatar was added and presented as a teammate rowing in the same virtual environment.

The main task was to row as far as possible in 9 minutes. The study measured distance, power output, stroke rate, heart rate, ratings of perceived exertion, affect, motivation, enjoyment, and related exercise questionnaires.

The main findings were:

- Both VR groups rowed farther than the no-VR group.
- Both VR groups produced higher power output than the no-VR group.
- The companion-VR group rowed farther than the individual-VR group.
- The companion-VR group had a higher heart rate than the individual-VR group.
- The VR groups rated the task as more enjoyable than the no-VR group.
- The VR groups did not perceive themselves as exerting more physical effort than the no-VR group.

The authors interpret this as evidence that VR can improve performance and affective response during rowing exercise, and that the presence of another person/avatar in the virtual environment can further enhance performance.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

This paper is useful because it is a direct predecessor in the broad space of VR rowing. It shows that rowing ergometers have been connected to virtual rowing environments before, and that a visible virtual rowing avatar can be used in a projected display setup.

It is especially useful for a related-work sentence like:

> Prior VR rowing studies have shown that virtual rowing environments and social companions can increase rowing distance, power output, and enjoyment compared with rowing without VR.

It also helps distinguish the current thesis:

- Murray et al. examine motivation/performance effects of projected VR rowing and companion presence.
- The present thesis examines embodiment/congruence effects of different avatar/action mappings in XR rowing.
- Murray et al. used a third-person avatar from behind, whereas the present thesis is concerned with embodied first-person/egocentric hand/body mapping and the tradeoff between erg-centered congruence and boat-centered sport depiction.

This makes the paper useful but not core theory. It is close enough to read because it is actual VR rowing with a third-person avatar and performance/enjoyment outcomes.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- VR rowing was implemented with a Concept2 Model D ergometer and Netathlon 2 XF projected environment.
- Participants rowed for 9 minutes with the PM3 monitor hidden.
- VR groups saw a projected rowing course; the avatar’s stroke timing matched ergometer strokes.
- The companion condition added another boat/avatar, presented as a teammate.
- Both VR groups rowed farther and produced higher power than no-VR.
- Companion VR further increased distance relative to individual VR.
- Companion VR produced higher heart rate than individual VR.
- VR increased enjoyment compared with no-VR.
- VR performance gains did not require participants to report higher perceived exertion.
- The study supports VR rowing as motivational/performance-related related work, not as direct embodiment evidence.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### VR rowing related work

High relevance. This is one of the closer related-work sources because it uses an ergometer connected to a virtual rowing environment.

### Third-person avatar rowing

High relevance for related work. The study used a third-person rear-view rowing avatar whose stroke timing matched the ergometer. This is directly useful as an example that prior VR rowing work has used avatar rowing, but not necessarily embodied first-person avatar ownership.

### Performance and motivation

High relevance. The study found increased distance and power output in VR rowing, and further performance gains with a companion avatar.

### Enjoyment / affective response

High relevance for app framing. The VR conditions were more enjoyable without higher perceived exertion, which is useful for explaining why virtual rowing may be compelling even if the thesis does not evaluate long-term adherence.

### Social presence / companion effects

High relevance if the thesis/app discusses multiplayer, team rowing, leader rowing, or club/social rowing. The paper supports the idea that virtual others can affect exertion.

### Presence

Moderate relevance. The paper uses VR/presence framing, but it is not your best source for place illusion or plausibility. Use Slater/Skarbez for those definitions.

### Embodiment

Low-to-moderate relevance. The paper includes a virtual avatar matched to the rower’s stroke timing, but it does not evaluate body ownership, agency, self-location, or the avatar as the user’s body.

### Erg-centered vs boat-centered mode

Low direct relevance. The setup is ergometer-based and virtual-rowing themed, but it does not compare a physically congruent erg-centered avatar against a sport-faithful boat/oar depiction.

### Sport-action realism

Moderate relevance as related work. The virtual environment used rowing scenery and avatar rowing, but it was not designed around detailed oar/IK congruence.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

Indirectly relevant. The study links the ergometer to virtual rowing movement and synchronizes avatar stroke timing with the real erg stroke. This supports the general feasibility of erg-controlled virtual rowing displays, but it does not test the kind of precise handle/seat/body congruence in the erg-centered thesis mode.

### Boat-centered mode

Indirectly relevant. The study presents rowing as a boat/course environment, but not as a detailed oar-centered biomechanical representation. It is more of a projected virtual rowing course with an avatar/boat, not a detailed boat-centered embodiment mapping.

### Core tradeoff

This paper does not address the core tradeoff directly. Its contribution is to show that virtual rowing environments and companion avatars can change performance and enjoyment. The current thesis can build on this related work but asks a different question: how the body/action representation itself affects embodiment.

## What not to overclaim

- Do not claim this paper proves avatar embodiment in VR rowing.
- Do not claim this paper measures body ownership, agency, or self-location.
- Do not claim it compares erg-centered and boat-centered representation.
- Do not claim it validates first-person embodied rowing.
- Do not claim VR rowing always improves performance; the study used a specific short 9-minute task and specific participants.
- Do not claim companion avatars always improve performance; this study used a particular teammate/cover-story manipulation.
- Do not claim increased heart rate or power means better technique.
- Do not use it as evidence that VR teaches rowing technique.
- Do not treat projected screen VR as equivalent to HMD XR embodiment.

## Candidate glossary wording

### Companion VR rowing

Companion VR rowing refers to a virtual rowing setup in which the user rows alongside another represented rower or boat in the virtual environment. Murray et al. found that adding a companion avatar to a projected VR rowing task increased performance compared with rowing alone in VR, suggesting that virtual others can influence exertion and motivation.

### Screen-based / projected VR rowing

Screen-based or projected VR rowing uses an ergometer connected to a virtual rowing environment displayed on a monitor, wall, projector, or large screen rather than through a head-mounted display. Murray et al.’s setup belongs to this category: it provided virtual rowing scenery and avatar feedback, but it was not an HMD-based embodied XR system.

### Third-person rowing avatar

A third-person rowing avatar is a virtual rower viewed from outside the body, often from behind. In Murray et al.’s VR rowing setup, the participant saw a rowing avatar from behind, and the avatar’s stroke timing matched the participant’s ergometer strokes. This differs from a first-person embodied avatar, where the virtual body is intended to be experienced as the user’s own body.
