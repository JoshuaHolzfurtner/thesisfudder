# Thesis notes — Palmisano et al. 2015, “Future challenges for vection research”

## Citation identity

Palmisano, S., Allison, R. S., Schira, M. M., & Barry, R. J. (2015). *Future challenges for vection research: definitions, functional significance, measures, and neural bases*. Frontiers in Psychology, 6, Article 193. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00193

## What this paper is

This is a review / conceptual challenge paper about vection. It does not study rowing, cycling, exergames, avatars, or embodiment directly. Its purpose is to review and clarify the state of vection research, especially four major challenges:

1. how vection should be defined,
2. whether vection has functional significance,
3. how vection should be measured,
4. what is known about the neural basis of vection.

For the XR rowing thesis, this is a definition and conceptual support paper. It should be used to explain what “vection” means and why optic-flow-based virtual locomotion can be discussed as more than just scenery moving on screen.

## Content summary

Palmisano et al. explain that vection has traditionally meant a visual illusion of self-motion in a stationary observer. A classic example is a person sitting still inside a visually moving environment, such as a rotating drum, and feeling as if they themselves are moving or rotating. The paper notes that this traditional definition is still common, but that the term has become broader over time.

The authors identify four definitions used in the literature:

1. Vection as a visual illusion of self-motion in a stationary observer.
2. Vection as an illusion of self-motion, whether visual or non-visual.
3. Vection as visually mediated self-motion perception, whether real or illusory.
4. Vection as the broad conscious subjective experience of self-motion.

Their central synthesis is that the common feature across these definitions is the conscious subjective experience of self-motion.

The paper then discusses whether vection is functionally meaningful or merely an epiphenomenon. Some researchers treat vection as a delayed conscious feeling that may appear after useful motion information has already been processed. Palmisano et al. argue that this is not settled. They review evidence suggesting vection may matter for self-motion judgments, steering/control, navigation, and spatial orientation, especially in virtual environments.

The paper also discusses the difficulty of measuring vection. Because vection is subjective, it is often measured with self-report: occurrence, onset, duration, and strength. But subjective reports can be affected by instructions, expectations, uncertainty, or confusion with discomfort/motion sickness. The authors therefore argue for auxiliary/objective indicators, including eye movements, EEG, postural responses, and behavioral measures. These should not replace self-report, but can help validate whether a study is really measuring self-motion experience.

Finally, the paper reviews neural-basis research. The authors argue that many brain-imaging studies use self-motion-consistent optic flow but do not verify whether participants actually experienced vection. Therefore, such studies may be measuring visual motion or optic-flow processing rather than the conscious experience of self-motion. The paper concludes that self-motion perception is multisensory and that future work should not study vision in isolation.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

This paper is useful because XR rowing turns stationary ergometer effort into apparent movement through a virtual rowing world. The user is not physically travelling down a river, but the system can show moving water, shoreline, buoys, competitors, boat response, and route progression. These visual cues can provide optic flow. If the user experiences these cues as self-motion, the relevant term is vection.

The paper helps separate three concepts:

- Optic flow: the visual motion pattern.
- Vection: the conscious subjective experience or illusion of self-motion.
- Embodiment: the relation between the user and the virtual body, including self-location, agency, and body ownership.

This distinction is important because the thesis should not claim that optic flow automatically creates embodiment. Vection may explain the felt locomotion layer of the app, while embodiment explains the virtual-body layer.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- Vection is most commonly used to refer to visually induced illusions of self-motion in physically stationary observers.
- The term has also been used more broadly for non-visual self-motion illusions, visually mediated self-motion, and general subjective self-motion experience.
- The shared core across definitions is conscious subjective self-motion experience.
- Optic flow can induce compelling illusions of self-motion, especially when it is large-field, dense, enveloping, or multisensory-consistent.
- Vection should not be equated with optic flow. Optic flow is the visual cue; vection is the felt self-motion experience.
- The functional role of vection is still debated. It may be more than an epiphenomenon, especially for spatial orientation in virtual environments, but the evidence base remains incomplete.
- Many self-motion simulation studies do not check whether vection actually occurred.
- Traditional vection measurement relies heavily on self-report, which is natural but imperfect.
- Auxiliary/objective indicators may include eye movements, EEG, postural responses, and behavioral measures.
- Objective indicators should support, not replace, subjective report because vection is still a subjective experience.
- Neural studies of self-motion are difficult to interpret when they do not verify whether participants experienced vection.
- Self-motion perception is multisensory, involving visual, vestibular, somatosensory, proprioceptive, and auditory systems.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### Optic flow

Very relevant. Optic flow is the visual movement pattern that can support vection. In XR rowing, optic-flow cues may come from moving water, shoreline, buoys, environmental texture, boat motion, and forward route progression.

### Vection

High relevance for definition. This paper is the best current core source in the bundle for saying what vection is. Use it to define vection as the conscious subjective experience or illusion of self-motion.

### Virtual locomotion

Relevant. XR rowing is a virtual locomotion system because stationary physical effort is mapped into virtual movement through a world. Vection helps explain how this virtual movement can feel spatially meaningful.

### Anti-Sisyphus effect

Useful as conceptual support. The Anti-Sisyphus effect is not a scientific term from this paper. But the paper helps explain one underlying perceptual mechanism: stationary effort can feel less like exertion in place when optic-flow cues and world movement support a sense of moving through space.

### Presence / spatial orientation

Relevant but not identical. The paper suggests vection may matter for spatial orientation in virtual environments. However, vection is not the same as presence. Presence is broader; vection is specifically the felt experience of self-motion.

### Embodiment

Indirect relevance only. Vection is distinct from embodiment. It does not prove body ownership, agency, or self-location in the avatar sense. It supports the locomotion/world-motion layer, not the avatar-body ownership layer.

### Sense of agency

Indirect relevance. If rowing effort controls visual movement through the environment, users may feel that their action causes locomotion. But this is action-outcome congruence / agency, not vection by itself.

### Cybersickness / comfort

Some relevance. The paper mentions that self-motion simulations can have side effects such as disorientation and motion sickness, and that confusing discomfort with vection is a measurement concern. Use cautiously.

### Evaluation / questionnaire design

Useful if the thesis includes vection as a secondary questionnaire or future-work measure. The paper supports asking about self-motion experience separately from embodiment. It also supports not relying only on one subjective item if vection becomes a serious dependent variable.

Possible questionnaire-style items:
- I felt as if I was moving through the virtual rowing environment.
- The movement of the water and shoreline made me feel like I was travelling forward.
- I saw the world moving, but I did not feel as if I was moving. [reverse / distinction item]
- The virtual movement felt like it was caused by my rowing.
- The rowing environment gave me a convincing sense of forward motion.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

In the erg-centered mode, vection may be supported by virtual world motion and effort-coupled route progression. The avatar and handle are tightly mapped to the real erg, but the user is still physically stationary. Vection can explain the perceptual layer by which the erg workout may feel like movement through a virtual rowing world.

The paper does not tell us whether high handle/body congruence increases vection. If anything, that would be an empirical question for the thesis or future work.

### Boat-centered mode

In the boat-centered mode, vection may be especially relevant because the user sees a more explicit on-water rowing scene: boat, oars, water, shore, and forward travel. This mode may provide stronger sport-action cues for interpreting visual motion as boat locomotion.

However, the paper does not prove that boat-centered rowing creates more vection than erg-centered rowing. It only supplies the concept and measurement caution.

### Core tradeoff

Vection belongs to the app’s world-motion / locomotion layer. The central thesis tradeoff is bodily/device congruence versus sport-action congruence. Vection may interact with this tradeoff, but it is not the same construct.

Possible future research question:
- Does a more boat-centered visual representation increase vection because the forward motion is interpreted as on-water travel?
- Or does erg-centered high-congruence support stronger action-outcome control, making locomotion feel more self-caused?

This is useful for future work, not necessarily for the main thesis scope.

## What not to overclaim

- Do not claim optic flow equals vection.
- Do not claim vection equals presence.
- Do not claim vection equals embodiment.
- Do not claim vection proves body ownership.
- Do not claim vection is always beneficial.
- Do not claim stronger vection automatically means better XR rowing.
- Do not claim the rowing prototype created vection unless it is measured or explicitly reported.
- Do not claim vection is a solved concept; the paper is explicitly about unresolved challenges.
- Do not treat objective indicators such as postural sway as direct vection meters.
- Do not expand the thesis into a full vection literature review unless vection becomes a measured dependent variable.

## Candidate thesis paragraph

Vection provides a useful term for the locomotion layer of XR rowing. Palmisano et al. describe vection as centering on the conscious subjective experience of self-motion, traditionally in the form of a visual illusion of self-motion in a stationary observer. In XR rowing, the user remains physically on an ergometer while visual cues such as moving water, shoreline motion, boat response, and route progression may support the impression of travelling through a rowing environment. This should be distinguished from embodiment: vection concerns felt self-motion through space, whereas embodiment concerns the relation between the user and the virtual body. Therefore, vection can explain part of the app’s “stationary effort becomes locomotion” effect, but it should not be treated as evidence for body ownership or avatar agency by itself.

Vection describes the conscious experience or illusion of self-motion and is useful for explaining how optic-flow cues in XR rowing may make stationary ergometer exercise feel like movement through a virtual rowing environment.

## Candidate glossary wording

### Vection

Vection is the conscious subjective experience or illusion of self-motion. In the classic case, a physically stationary observer sees a moving or simulated moving environment and feels as if they themselves are moving. In XR rowing, vection may be supported when the user rows on a stationary ergometer while the water, shoreline, boat, and virtual route move in a way that suggests forward travel.

Vection should be distinguished from optic flow. Optic flow is the visual motion pattern; vection is the felt self-motion experience that may result from such cues. Vection should also be distinguished from embodiment. Vection concerns movement through space, whereas embodiment concerns the user’s relation to the virtual body.

### Optic flow and vection

Optic-flow cues can support vection, but they do not guarantee it. A user may see the virtual world moving without strongly feeling self-motion. Therefore, in thesis language it is safer to say that the rowing environment provides optic-flow cues that may support vection, unless vection is directly measured.

### Vection in XR rowing

In XR rowing, vection is relevant because the user’s body stays in place while the virtual rowing scene depicts movement through a world. If the moving water and shoreline are interpreted as self-motion, the workout can feel less like stationary repetition and more like travelling through a rowing environment. This can support the app’s “anti-Sisyphus” design logic, but it is not identical to embodiment or agency.
