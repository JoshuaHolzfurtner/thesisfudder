# Thesis notes — Ma & Hommel 2013, “The virtual-hand illusion: effects of impact and threat on perceived ownership and affective resonance”

## Citation identity

Ma, K., & Hommel, B. (2013). *The virtual-hand illusion: effects of impact and threat on perceived ownership and affective resonance*. Frontiers in Psychology, 4, Article 604. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00604

## What this paper is

This is an original experimental paper on the virtual-hand illusion. It tests how perceived ownership of a virtual hand and physiological affective response relate when the virtual hand is either impacted by a harmless object or threatened by a more severe object.

It is not a rowing paper, not a full-body avatar paper, and not an XR sports paper. Its value for the XR rowing thesis is methodological: it warns that physiological threat responses to a virtual body part should not be treated as a pure body-ownership measure. Threats that are too intense may trigger affective defense responses even when ownership is weak.

For the XR rowing thesis, this is useful if the evaluation includes a rubber-hand-style probe such as a rubber hammer smackdown, collision, oar/hand impact, or other mild threat/impact at the end of a condition.

## Content summary

Ma and Hommel first replicated the virtual-hand illusion. Participants wore a data glove and controlled a virtual hand shown on a screen. In the synchronous condition, the virtual hand moved in synchrony with the real hand. In the asynchronous condition, the virtual hand moved with a delay. The authors found stronger ownership ratings in the synchronous condition, showing that their setup could induce a virtual-hand illusion.

The main experiment then compared two types of events directed at the virtual hand:

1. Impact condition:
   - a virtual ball hit the virtual hand,
   - the contact was paired with vibration feedback,
   - the event was noticeable but not strongly threatening.

2. Threat condition:
   - a virtual knife cut the virtual hand,
   - the contact was paired with vibration feedback,
   - blood appeared from the cut,
   - the event was intended as a stronger threat.

The authors measured subjective virtual-hand ownership using questionnaire items adapted from rubber-hand illusion studies. They also measured affective resonance using skin conductance response (SCR), a physiological arousal measure.

The questionnaire results were straightforward: ownership was stronger in synchronous than asynchronous conditions. Event type did not meaningfully change ownership. In other words, the virtual hand felt more owned when it moved in sync with the participant’s real hand, regardless of whether it was hit by a ball or cut by a knife.

The SCR results were the important part. For the ball impact condition, SCR was higher in the synchronous condition than in the asynchronous condition. This suggests that mild impact responses were ownership-dependent: participants reacted more when the impacted hand felt more like their own.

For the knife threat condition, SCR was high in both synchronous and asynchronous conditions. The difference between synchronous and asynchronous was no longer significant. This suggests that the severe threat triggered affective response even when ownership was weaker.

The authors conclude that body ownership and affective resonance can be dissociated. Affective response to mild impact may depend more on top-down ownership interpretation, while response to severe threat may be triggered more directly, possibly through a faster bottom-up defensive pathway. Therefore, a strong threat response does not necessarily prove that the threatened virtual hand was experienced as owned.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

This paper is useful if the thesis uses or discusses a threat/impact probe at the end of the XR rowing conditions.

A common embodiment idea is:
- if users flinch, show SCR, or react when the virtual hand/body is threatened, that may indicate ownership.

Ma and Hommel complicate this. They show that threat response depends on the severity and meaning of the event:
- a mild impact seems more ownership-sensitive,
- a severe threat can trigger response even without strong ownership.

For the rowing thesis, this supports using a **mild, non-gory, non-extreme impact** if the goal is to probe embodiment/ownership differences. A “rubber hammer smackdown,” soft object impact, oar bump, or low-danger collision may be more diagnostically useful than a violent or injury-like event. If the event is too harsh, almost everyone may react because the stimulus is threatening, not because the virtual hand/body is owned.

This is especially relevant because the thesis may compare two modes:
1. Erg-centered mode:
   - high real/virtual hand/handle congruence.
2. Boat-centered mode:
   - more sport-realistic oars but possible hand/oar mapping offset.

A mild impact probe might reveal whether the virtual hands/body are treated as self-related in each mode. A severe threat might blur the distinction.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- The study replicated the virtual-hand illusion using synchronous virtual hand movement.
- Synchronous movement produced stronger virtual-hand ownership than asynchronous movement.
- Ownership ratings were not meaningfully changed by whether the hand was hit by a ball or cut by a knife.
- SCR/affective response in the mild impact condition was stronger when ownership was stronger.
- SCR/affective response in the knife threat condition was high regardless of synchronicity/ownership.
- The authors argue that impact responses may depend on top-down ownership interpretation.
- Strong threat responses may be triggered more directly and may not require ownership.
- Therefore, affective resonance and body ownership are related but dissociable.
- Threat/SCR measures should not be treated as pure ownership measures.
- The severity and meaning of the stimulus matter for interpreting threat-response probes.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### Body ownership

High relevance if using threat/impact probes. The paper shows that questionnaire-based ownership and physiological affective response do not always behave the same way.

### Affective resonance

High relevance. Affective resonance means the participant reacts emotionally/physiologically to something happening to the virtual body part. This paper shows that affective resonance can be ownership-mediated for mild impact but ownership-independent for stronger threat.

### Threat response

High relevance and caution. A severe threat may cause a response even if the virtual hand is not owned. Therefore, threat response should be interpreted as auxiliary evidence, not as a clean measure of ownership.

### Impact response

Very relevant for your “rubber hammer smackdown” idea. A mild impact may be more useful than a severe injury-like threat because it is less likely to trigger a broad defensive response in everyone.

### SCR / skin conductance

Relevant but optional. The paper uses SCR as a physiological arousal measure. If the thesis does not measure SCR, the paper can still support qualitative caution about threat/impact interpretation.

### Sense of embodiment

Indirect relevance. The paper concerns virtual-hand ownership and affective response, not full sense of embodiment. It does not directly measure agency, self-location, or full-body avatar embodiment.

### Erg-centered mode

If the erg-centered mode produces stronger body ownership over virtual hands, a mild impact to the virtual hand/handle may produce stronger affective/behavioral response than in a less congruent condition. But this must be tested; the paper does not prove it for rowing.

### Boat-centered mode

If the boat-centered mode uses offset virtual oar hands, a mild impact may help test whether participants treat the visible virtual hand/oar contact as self-relevant. However, a severe threat could produce response regardless of ownership and therefore would be less diagnostic.

### Evaluation design

High relevance. The paper supports choosing probe severity carefully. If using an end-of-condition embodiment probe, prefer mild, safe, ethically comfortable, non-injury-like impact over aggressive cutting/stabbing/chopping imagery.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

A mild impact probe may be useful here because the erg-centered mode has direct real/virtual handle and hand congruence. If participants see a rubber hammer or ball hit the virtual hand/handle exactly where their real contact is represented, the event may be interpreted as more self-relevant.

However, this should still be measured alongside ownership/agency/location questionnaire items. A visible reaction alone is not enough.

### Boat-centered mode

The boat-centered mode may show oars and hands that do not perfectly match the real central erg handle. A mild impact probe could reveal whether the virtual hands/oars are still treated as self-relevant or tool-relevant. But if the event is too severe, the difference between modes may disappear because strong threat responses can occur without ownership.

### Core tradeoff

This paper helps refine the evaluation logic:
- If the probe is mild, differences may reflect ownership/self-relevance more clearly.
- If the probe is severe, differences may reflect generic threat processing.
- Therefore, the probe should match the construct being tested.

In other words: do not use a “master clown chopping off the avatar hand” if the goal is to measure subtle embodiment differences. It may be memorable, but it may be too strong and too non-specific.

## What not to overclaim

- Do not claim SCR/threat response proves body ownership.
- Do not claim no ownership is needed for all affective responses; the paper distinguishes impact from severe threat.
- Do not claim a knife-threat result transfers directly to XR rowing.
- Do not claim a mild rubber hammer response is a validated rowing embodiment measure.
- Do not use violent/gory threat as a clean ownership probe.
- Do not treat affective resonance, ownership, agency, and self-location as the same construct.
- Do not rely on threat/impact response alone; pair it with questionnaire items.
- Do not assume a physical flinch means body ownership; it may reflect surprise, startle, threat, or task expectation.

## Candidate thesis paragraph

Ma and Hommel show that affective responses to virtual body events depend on the severity and meaning of the event. In their virtual-hand illusion study, synchronous movement increased subjective ownership of the virtual hand. For a mild impact event, skin conductance responses were stronger when the virtual hand was synchronously controlled and therefore more strongly owned. For a more severe knife threat, however, physiological responses were high even in the asynchronous condition, suggesting that strong threat responses can occur without strong ownership. For the XR rowing evaluation, this implies that an end-of-condition impact probe should remain mild if it is intended to differentiate self-relevance or ownership between avatar mappings. A severe or gory threat would be less diagnostic because it may trigger a general defensive response.

## Candidate glossary wording

### Affective resonance

Affective resonance is an emotional or physiological response to something happening to a virtual or observed body part. In embodiment research, it can be used as auxiliary evidence that a virtual body part is treated as self-relevant. However, Ma and Hommel show that affective resonance is not identical to body ownership: mild impact responses may depend on ownership, while severe threats can trigger responses even without strong ownership.

### Threat response

A threat response is a defensive, emotional, or physiological reaction to a perceived danger to the body or a body-like object. In XR embodiment studies, a threat to a virtual hand or body may produce a reaction, but this does not automatically prove ownership. If the threat is too severe, participants may respond because the event is threatening in general, not because the virtual hand feels like their own.

### Mild impact probe

A mild impact probe is a non-gory, non-dangerous event directed at a virtual body part, such as a ball hit, rubber hammer tap, or object bump. Compared with severe threat, it may be more useful for detecting ownership-related differences because it is less likely to trigger a general defensive response in all participants.

## Codex / agent note

Use this paper if the thesis includes or discusses an end-of-condition impact/threat probe. It is a caution source, not a core embodiment theory source.

Core use:
- support using a mild impact probe instead of severe threat,
- warn that strong threat responses are not pure ownership evidence,
- pair any threat/impact response with questionnaires,
- distinguish affective resonance from ownership.

Best design implication:
- If adding a “rubber hammer smackdown,” keep it mild, non-gory, predictable enough to be ethical, but still visually directed at the relevant virtual hand/handle/body part.
- Do not use cutting, stabbing, or gore if the goal is subtle ownership differentiation.
- Record it as exploratory/auxiliary unless you have a full physiological setup.

Do not use this paper:
- as rowing evidence,
- as proof that threat probes validate ownership,
- as proof that a flinch equals embodiment,
- as proof that severe threat is a better test.

Suggested thesis logic:
1. Questionnaires remain the main ownership/agency/location measure.
2. A mild impact probe may serve as auxiliary self-relevance evidence.
3. Severe threat is avoided because it may trigger generic defensive response independent of ownership.
