# Thesis notes — Pilacinski, Metzler & Klaes (2023), “Phantom touch illusion, an unexpected phenomenological effect of tactile gating in the absence of tactile stimulation”

## Citation identity

Pilacinski, A., Metzler, M., & Klaes, C. (2023). Phantom touch illusion, an unexpected phenomenological effect of tactile gating in the absence of tactile stimulation. *Scientific Reports*, 13, 15453.

## What this paper is

This paper reports a laboratory VR effect the authors call the “phantom touch illusion” (PTI).

Participants wore an Oculus Quest 2 in hand-tracking mode and saw virtual hands. They used a virtual stick to stroke their virtual hand while being instructed to keep their real hands apart and avoid physical contact. Despite no real tactile stimulation at the touched hand, many participants reported a touch-like or tingling sensation at the virtual contact location.

The authors interpret PTI as potentially related to tactile gating during self-touch: the nervous system predicts tactile consequences of self-generated touch, but in VR there is no real tactile input to attenuate. They suggest the resulting process may become consciously experienced as a tingling/touch-like sensation.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

### 1) Most participants reported a touch-like sensation without physical touch

All but four of 36 participants reported tactile sensation when using the virtual stick to touch their virtual hand.

The sensation was commonly described as:
- tingling
- static
- prickle
- electric
- wind-like

Participants also reported that the sensation moved consistently with the position of the virtual stick on the virtual hand.

### 2) The effect was stronger than control conditions

The authors compared the VR condition against control conditions using a laser pointer without the same realistic visual-object contact.

The proportion of participants reporting sensation was significantly higher in the main VR condition than in the control condition without visual stimulation. “Tingling” descriptions were also much more common in the main VR condition than in both laser control conditions.

This makes the paper more credible than pure social-VR anecdote: it is still self-report, but the effect was produced in a controlled lab study and compared against controls.

### 3) Invisible / inferred body parts also produced PTI

A key result for your footnote: many participants also reported the sensation when touching an invisible forearm.

This matters because it suggests the effect is not purely driven by seeing a virtual surface being contacted. The authors argue that tactile gating may involve multisensory and top-down body-schema input, not just direct visual input.

### 4) The authors distinguish PTI from classic embodiment illusions

They explicitly say PTI is different from rubber-hand-style embodiment illusions.

Classic RHI/virtual hand illusions usually involve real tactile stimulation that is visually transferred to an artificial/virtual body part. PTI, by contrast, involves a touch-like sensation without physical tactile stimulation.

For the thesis:
- Do not treat PTI as a standard ownership illusion.
- Use it as a side note that VR can sometimes elicit touch-like sensations from visual/inferred self-touch alone.

### 5) The authors connect the term to VR-community reports

The authors say they used the term “phantom touch illusion” partly to relate it to apparently similar phantom-touch reports in the VR community.

This is the connection to social-VR “phantom sense,” but the paper itself is a lab study of hand/forearm self-touch, not a study of social-VR tails/ears.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

This is not core thesis theory, but it is credible footnote material.

Its value:
- It gives a lab-observed version of a phenomenon often discussed anecdotally in social VR.
- It supports a cautious statement that immersive VR can elicit touch-like sensations without corresponding physical touch.
- It strengthens the speculative outlook that virtual body/tool structures may sometimes produce body-like sensations, especially when action, vision, and body schema align.

For your rowing thesis, the safe link is not:
“virtual oars become phantom limbs.”

The safe link is:
“VR embodiment and body-schema processes can sometimes generate body-like sensory experiences even without direct physical stimulation; future work could investigate whether oar-constrained avatar arms or virtual rowing tools produce similar body-tool experiences.”

## Specific footnote relevance

This paper is more footnote-worthy than social-VR anecdotes because:
- it is peer-reviewed,
- it is a Scientific Reports paper,
- it used a controlled VR setup,
- it tested 36 participants,
- it included control conditions,
- it reports an effect even for invisible/inferred forearm parts.

However, it is still based on subjective reports, not direct physiological detection of a phantom limb-like body part.

## Suggested long footnote for thesis

“An adjacent phenomenon is the ‘phantom touch illusion’ reported by Pilacinski et al. (2023), in which participants in immersive VR reported tingling or touch-like sensations at locations where a virtual stick contacted their virtual hand, despite no physical tactile stimulation. The effect was also reported when participants touched invisible/inferred forearm regions, which the authors interpret as evidence that tactile gating may involve body-schema and top-down multisensory processes rather than vision alone. This should not be confused with clinical phantom-limb phenomena or treated as proof that virtual tools become body parts. However, it suggests that VR can sometimes elicit body-like sensory experiences from visually and action-based self-touch, making it a relevant speculative pointer for future work on whether virtual oars or oar-constrained avatar arms can be experienced as body-tool extensions.”

## How this differs from social-VR phantom tails/ears

Social-VR “phantom sense” reports often refer to users feeling touch-like sensations on avatar features such as ears, tails, wings, or other non-biological body parts.

Pilacinski et al. does not test tails or ears. It tests virtual hand/forearm contact.

Therefore:
- Use Pilacinski et al. for the general claim that phantom touch-like sensations can occur in VR without real tactile stimulation.
- Use social-VR reports only if you specifically discuss community-reported phantom sense for non-biological avatar parts.
- Do not use Pilacinski et al. alone to claim evidence for phantom tails/ears.

## Relation to oar arms / virtual oars

A cautious thesis/outlook bridge:

In a future free-hand rowing mode, the user could move tracked hands while virtual oars are constrained between the hands and oarlocks. If the visual motion, action prediction, and body schema form a coherent loop, users might experience some body-like or pseudo-haptic qualities of the virtual oar/arm system. Pilacinski et al. does not prove this, but it makes such a question scientifically plausible enough to mention as future work.

Potential future-work phrasing:

“Future research could examine whether visually coherent virtual-oar contact or oar-constrained hand motion can produce pseudo-haptic or phantom-touch-like experiences, analogous to the PTI observed for virtual self-touch, and whether such sensations influence agency or ownership in boat-centered XR rowing.”

## What not to overclaim

Do not call PTI “proven phantom limbs.”

Do not claim this establishes phantom tails or phantom ears.

Do not claim objective physiological evidence for the sensation; the paper primarily reports subjective verbal descriptions and ratings.

Do not treat PTI as identical to rubber hand illusion, body ownership, or sense of embodiment. The authors explicitly distinguish it from standard embodiment illusions.

Do not use it as core evidence for the main erg-vs-boat hypothesis. It belongs in a footnote, outlook, or “speculative adjacent work” paragraph.
