# Thesis notes — Harris et al. 2019, “Virtually the same? How impaired sensory information in virtual reality may disrupt vision for action”

Harris, D. J., Buckingham, G., Wilson, M. R., & Vine, S. J. (2019). *Virtually the same? How impaired sensory information in virtual reality may disrupt vision for action*. Experimental Brain Research, 237, 2761-2766. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-019-05642-8

## What this paper is

This is a short theoretical mini-review about how virtual reality may alter visually guided action. It is not an embodiment paper, not a rowing paper, and not a VR rowing study. Its value for the XR rowing thesis is that it gives a precise perception-action caution:

> VR can look convincing while still providing altered depth cues, conflicting visual information, and limited haptic feedback; therefore, actions performed in VR may not rely on the same online control processes as actions performed in the real world.

The authors discuss:

- vision for action,
- dorsal versus ventral processing,
- depth cues in head-mounted VR,
- vergence-accommodation conflict,
- haptic feedback limitations,
- pantomimed reaching and virtual object interaction,
- implications for training and transfer.

For the thesis, this is a **green source** because it strengthens the claim that “realism” in XR rowing cannot be reduced to visual sport depiction.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

The thesis compares two representation modes:

1. **Erg-centered / high-congruence mode**
   - the user rows on a real ergometer,
   - the avatar follows the real handle, seat, and body movement closely,
   - the mode prioritizes visuomotor, temporal, spatial, haptic/proprioceptive, and agency congruence.

2. **Boat-centered / sport-depiction mode**
   - the user still rows on an ergometer,
   - the virtual avatar appears to row in a boat with oars, oarlocks, blades, and water movement,
   - the mode prioritizes sport-action realism,
   - it introduces mismatch because the virtual oar/hand path and blade-water logic cannot perfectly match the real ergometer handle path and force loop.

Harris et al. are directly useful because they warn that VR sport/action should not be treated as equivalent to real-world action just because it is visually presented in 3D. For XR rowing, this means:

- a boat-centered mode may look more like on-water rowing,
- but the user’s haptic/proprioceptive/action loop remains ergometer-based,
- and the virtual oars/blades/water may not provide the sensory information needed for real oar-water action control.

This supports the thesis’s core distinction:

> Boat-centered mode may increase visual/contextual sport realism, while erg-centered mode may preserve the more immediate real action loop.

## Content summary

Harris et al. start from the problem that VR is increasingly used for training and research, including visually guided motor skills, but the basic perceptual effects of acting in virtual environments remain insufficiently understood. They argue that head-mounted VR presents depth information artificially. Although the user sees stereoscopic images, the display is still based on screens, and visual depth cues can be degraded or placed in conflict.

The paper focuses on the distinction between:

- **vision for perception**, associated mainly with ventral processing and object identification;
- **vision for action**, associated mainly with dorsal processing and real-time movement control.

Their central concern is that VR may shift actions away from normal dorsal online control toward more ventral, cognitively mediated control. In real-world action, depth cues, haptic feedback, and object contact support fast and efficient online movement control. In VR, some of these cues are impaired or missing. This may make virtual actions more similar to pantomimed or visually guided movements without endpoint contact, which can differ from natural real-world actions.

The authors discuss two main sensory issues:

1. **Depth cue problems**
   - VR can impair distance estimation.
   - Vergence and accommodation can conflict in HMDs.
   - Binocular depth cues may be less reliable.
   - Users may rely more on ventral/monocular processing than in real-world action.

2. **Haptic feedback limitations**
   - Current VR systems often lack realistic tactile/force feedback.
   - Controllers may vibrate but do not reproduce object-specific tactile properties or resistance.
   - Lack of endpoint tactile feedback can disrupt normal reaching/grasping control.
   - Actions toward virtual targets may resemble pantomimed movements.

The conclusion is not that VR is useless for action or training. Rather, the authors argue that VR action can differ from real-world action in important ways, and these differences should be taken seriously when designing experiments, training systems, or claims about transfer.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- VR is promising for experimentation and training, but its perceptual environment differs from the real world.
- Head-mounted VR can impair or conflict depth cues.
- Vergence-accommodation conflict is a key issue in HMDs.
- Binocular information is important for dorsal online action control.
- If binocular and haptic cues are impaired, users may rely more on ventral/cognitive control.
- Lack of realistic haptic feedback can disrupt natural action control.
- Virtual actions can resemble pantomimed actions toward imagined or virtual targets.
- Pantomimed or virtual reaching may differ kinematically from real-world reaching.
- These differences matter for visually guided motor skills and training transfer.
- The paper supports caution when assuming that VR sport actions are “virtually the same” as real sport actions.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### Visuomotor congruence

High relevance. The paper supports the idea that visually guided action in VR depends on the quality and reliability of sensory information, not simply on visual appearance. In XR rowing, visuomotor congruence is not only whether the avatar looks like rowing, but whether the seen movement fits the user’s performed movement.

### Haptic / force congruence

Very high relevance. Harris et al. emphasize that lack of realistic haptic information can alter action control. This maps directly to rowing: the ergometer supplies a real handle and resistance, but the boat-centered visual representation depicts a different force system involving oars, blades, oarlocks, and water.

### Biomechanical fidelity

High relevance. The paper does not use rowing-specific biomechanical language, but it supports the general claim that motor actions in VR may differ from real-world actions when sensory/action constraints differ. This connects well to Richlan’s fidelity vocabulary.

### Sport-action realism

High relevance as a caution. Sport-action realism should not be treated as visual realism alone. A rowing scene with oars may look sport-faithful but still lack the sensory and action-control conditions of on-water rowing.

### Agency

Moderate-high relevance. If action control feels mediated, delayed, pantomimed, or not supported by expected haptic/depth information, agency could plausibly be reduced. Use Waltemate and VEQ as stronger agency sources, and Harris as the perception-action explanation.

### Body ownership

Moderate relevance. Harris is not a body-ownership paper, but its haptic/depth/action-control argument can support why bodily congruence and action feedback might affect embodiment outcomes.

### Spatial alignment

Moderate relevance. The discussion of depth and distance cues is relevant to why spatial alignment in XR matters, but the paper is not about MR alignment procedures.

### Erg-centered mode

High relevance. The erg-centered mode may be less visually sport-realistic, but it preserves the actual action loop: the real handle, real seat, real body movement, and avatar movement are tightly coupled. Harris supports the idea that such action-relevant sensory information may matter more for action control than visual depiction alone.

### Boat-centered mode

Very high relevance as a caution. The boat-centered mode may look more like on-water rowing, but the user is not actually interacting with real oars/blades/water. Harris supports caution in assuming that this visual sport depiction creates action equivalence.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

The erg-centered mode can be described as preserving the user’s actual interaction constraints. The user pulls a real handle, feels real resistance, moves on a real seat, and sees an avatar movement closely matched to these actions. In Harris et al.’s terms, this mode may preserve more action-relevant sensory consistency than a purely visual sport depiction, even if it is less faithful to on-water rowing.

### Boat-centered mode

The boat-centered mode visually represents rowing as on-water sport action. However, the user’s real haptic and biomechanical experience remains erg-based. Harris et al. help explain why this matters: when depth cues, haptic feedback, and action constraints differ, VR actions may not recruit the same control processes as real-world actions. Therefore, the boat-centered mode should be described as a sport-depiction approximation, not as real on-water action equivalence.

### Core tradeoff

Harris sharpens the core tradeoff:

- **Boat-centered mode**: higher visual/contextual sport fidelity.
- **Erg-centered mode**: higher action/haptic/biomechanical congruence to the actual task being performed.

The thesis should ask which of these supports embodiment more strongly, rather than assuming visual sport realism is always better.

## Candidate thesis paragraph

Visual realism in VR does not necessarily imply action equivalence. Harris et al. argue that visually guided action in VR may differ from real-world action because depth information and haptic feedback are altered or absent, potentially shifting action control away from normal online vision-for-action mechanisms. This caution is important for XR rowing. A boat-centered representation may visually resemble on-water rowing more closely by showing oars, oarlocks, blades, and water movement, but the user still acts through an ergometer and does not receive real oar-water forces or the same handle trajectory. An erg-centered representation may therefore be less sport-faithful visually, but more congruent with the actual haptic and biomechanical action loop available to the user.

## Candidate glossary wording

### Vision for action

Vision for action refers to visual processing used to guide real-time movement, such as reaching, grasping, or interacting with objects. Harris et al. argue that VR may disrupt vision for action because depth cues and haptic feedback differ from real-world interaction.

### Depth cue conflict

Depth cue conflict occurs when visual cues that normally agree in the real world become inconsistent. In HMD-based VR, vergence and accommodation can be decoupled because simulated depth is shown on a fixed display surface. This may affect distance perception and action control.

### Haptic action congruence

Haptic action congruence means that felt touch, resistance, and force feedback match the visible virtual action. In XR rowing, the ergometer provides real handle resistance, but this force does not fully match the virtual oar/blade-water interaction shown in a boat-centered representation.

### Pantomimed action in VR

A virtual action can resemble pantomime when the user moves toward or interacts with a virtual object without the endpoint tactile and force feedback that would occur in the real world. This concept is relevant to virtual oars: unless real oar-water forces are reproduced, the displayed oar action remains partly visual rather than fully haptic.

## What not to overclaim

- Do not claim Harris et al. prove that boat-centered XR rowing will reduce embodiment.
- Do not claim VR action always fails to transfer to real action.
- Do not claim erg-centered mode is automatically better.
- Do not claim the paper is about rowing or embodiment directly.
- Do not treat dorsal/ventral processing as something measured in the thesis.
- Do not overstate neuroscience details unless directly checked in the paper.
- Do not use this as a questionnaire source.
- Do not use it as proof of sport performance outcomes.
