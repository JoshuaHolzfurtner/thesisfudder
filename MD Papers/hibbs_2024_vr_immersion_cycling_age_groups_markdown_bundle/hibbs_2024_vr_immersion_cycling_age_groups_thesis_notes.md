# Thesis notes — Hibbs et al. 2024, “Impact of virtual reality immersion on exercise performance and perceptions in young, middle-aged and older adults”

## Citation identity

Hibbs, A., Tempest, G., Hettinga, F., & Barry, G. (2024). *Impact of virtual reality immersion on exercise performance and perceptions in young, middle-aged and older adults*. PLOS ONE, 19(10), e0307683. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0307683

## What this paper is

This is an original experimental study on virtual-reality-supported stationary cycling. It compares different levels and formats of virtual immersion during cycling exercise and asks how they affect exercise performance and subjective experience across age groups.

Participants were young, middle-aged, and older adults, grouped as 18-35, 36-50, and 51-69 years. The study used 30 participants originally, balanced by sex across age groups, but one middle-aged participant was excluded after being unable to complete the high-VR condition because of nausea and dizziness.

The exercise protocol included two intensity levels:

- moderate steady-state cycling: 15 minutes at 60-75% maximum heart rate,
- high-intensity sprint cycling: 10 x 30-second sprints at 75-85% maximum heart rate, with 30-second rests.

The four experimental conditions were:

1. **High HMD VR**: an Oculus Quest 2 HMD with VZFit-style immersive cycling, where headset movement changed the view and the smart trainer responded to cycling effort.
2. **Low Screen VR**: Zwift displayed on a laptop screen in front of the cyclist; the smart trainer responded to cycling effort.
3. **Low Room VR**: a projected outdoor cycling video on three walls of an immersive room; importantly, this video was not linked to cycling effort.
4. **No VR**: stationary cycling without virtual feedback.

Performance measures included heart rate, cadence, distance, power output, and RPE. Subjective measures included the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES) and the Flow State Scale, including subscales such as sense of control, loss of self-consciousness, transformation of time, concentration, clear goals, unambiguous feedback, and autotelic experience.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

This paper is important because it directly complicates a simple “more immersion is better” argument. It is not about rowing and does not test embodiment in the strict sense of body ownership, self-location, and agency. However, it is highly relevant for the broader virtual sport / exergame framing because it compares HMD-based VR with lower-immersion, screen-based virtual cycling.

The most thesis-relevant finding is that the **Low Screen VR condition**, which used Zwift and responded to cycling effort, produced particularly favorable engagement outcomes. Participants reported stronger **sense of control** and **loss of self-consciousness** in Low Screen VR than in the other conditions. Meanwhile, HMD VR did not consistently improve performance or perceived effort, and it introduced nausea, dizziness, and balance-related concerns for some users.

For the XR rowing thesis, this supports a critical and non-salesy framing: XR rowing should not be presented as the obvious final or superior form of rowing applications. Instead, XR rowing can be framed as a design and research case for investigating which kinds of immersion, congruence, feedback, and realism matter. Display-based Zwift-like systems may remain preferable for many users because they can provide effort-coupled virtual locomotion while preserving comfort, control, visibility of the real body/equipment, and low setup friction.

This is especially useful when paired with Colombo et al. (2025). Colombo supports the positive role of immersive and interactive VR exercise for enjoyment, attention, spatial presence, and preference. Hibbs et al. provides the counterweight: higher immersion is not a simple ladder, and low-screen effort-coupled systems may be better tolerated and more control-supportive across age groups.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- The study did **not** find broad, consistent performance advantages for VR immersion. Condition effects on performance were limited. This matters because the thesis should avoid claiming that HMD VR automatically improves exercise performance.

- RPE did **not** significantly differ across VR and no-VR conditions during steady-state or sprint cycling. This matters because the paper does not support a claim that VR reliably makes exercise feel easier.

- During sprint cycling, heart rate was lower in the High VR condition compared with No VR, Low Screen VR, and Low Room VR, but this did not correspond to lower perceived effort or improved performance. This should be used cautiously, because the physiological interpretation is not straightforward.

- Exercise enjoyment did not show statistically significant condition effects, although there were trends toward increased enjoyment in VR conditions. The safest use is to say that the enjoyment pattern was favorable but not statistically decisive.

- Exercise engagement showed significant condition effects for **sense of control** and **loss of self-consciousness**, with the highest values in the **Low Screen VR** condition. This is one of the strongest thesis-relevant findings.

- For the middle-aged group, **transformation of time** differed by condition and was highest in High VR and Low Screen VR. This is relevant to flow/absorption, but should be framed carefully because it was age-group-specific and the paper notes that it is unclear whether time sped up or slowed down.

- The HMD condition caused problems for some participants. One participant could not complete the protocol because of nausea and dizziness; fewer than five others reported mild dizziness and nausea. Older participants also appeared to have more balance-maintenance issues during HMD cycling.

- The authors conclude that the low-screen, effort-responsive virtual cycling setup was well tolerated across age groups and resulted in favorable enjoyment and engagement scores. This is especially relevant to Zwift-like systems and desktop-first virtual sport.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### Virtual sport / exergaming

This is a strong background paper for virtual sport and exergaming. It examines how different virtual display conditions change cycling exercise experience. It is especially useful because it includes a real Zwift-like condition rather than only laboratory VR.

### Immersion

The paper directly addresses immersion level. It distinguishes high immersion HMD VR from low-immersion screen and room-projection formats. The key thesis point is that higher immersion did not automatically produce better outcomes. Low Screen VR produced the strongest results for some engagement dimensions.

### Screen-based virtual sport

This is one of the strongest uses of the paper. It supports the idea that display-based virtual sport is not merely a weak substitute for HMD XR. A low-screen, effort-coupled setup can be highly relevant for exercise engagement, control, and broad tolerability.

### Sense of control

Sense of control is the most directly useful Flow State Scale construct for the rowing thesis. It is not identical to sense of agency in embodiment theory, but it is conceptually adjacent. The Low Screen VR condition scored highest for sense of control. In rowing, this supports the importance of making the user feel that their physical effort clearly and comfortably controls the virtual activity.

### Flow / absorption

The study used the Flow State Scale and found effects on loss of self-consciousness and transformation of time. This is useful for the thesis if flow is discussed as a design outcome or adjacent experience variable. It should not be treated as a direct embodiment measure.

### Perceived effort

The paper is useful as a caution. It does not show that VR significantly lowers perceived exertion. If used alongside Colombo et al., it helps show that the relationship between VR, immersion, effort, and perception is still unsettled.

### Action-outcome congruence

The Low Screen condition is especially relevant because the smart trainer responded to cycling effort. This maps well to the thesis idea that stationary effort becomes meaningful when it produces virtual progress. However, the paper does not explicitly use the term action-outcome congruence, so this is an interpretation for thesis use.

### Optic flow / virtual locomotion

The Low Screen and High HMD conditions both involved virtual cycling environments responding to cycling effort. This supports the general logic of effort-coupled virtual locomotion. The paper does not focus on optic flow theory, but it is compatible with the thesis glossary framing of stationary effort producing virtual movement through a world.

### Presence / spatial presence

The paper discusses presence and engagement in the introduction, but its main subjective engagement tool is the Flow State Scale rather than a dedicated spatial presence questionnaire. Do not overuse this as a presence-measurement paper.

### Cybersickness / comfort

This paper is useful for comfort and safety discussion. It shows that HMD VR can introduce nausea, dizziness, and balance issues for some participants during cycling exercise. The authors recommend future work use the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire to quantify cybersickness more precisely.

### Age and user-base differences

This is an important strength. Unlike many VR exercise papers focused mostly on young adults, this paper includes young, middle-aged, and older adults. It supports the argument that user preference for HMD VR vs screen-based systems may differ by age, familiarity, comfort, and tolerance.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

The paper supports the erg-centered mode indirectly through the idea of **control-supportive, effort-coupled virtual locomotion**. In the Low Screen VR condition, the cyclist’s effort controlled the virtual cycling system. This is similar to an erg-centered rowing mode where the real rowing stroke, handle/seat motion, power, or pace directly drives avatar movement, boat speed, route progression, and feedback.

The strongest link is not body ownership but **sense of control** and **action-outcome consistency**. For the thesis, this supports the idea that tight, understandable mappings may be more important for user experience than simply maximizing display immersion.

### Boat-centered mode

The paper does not test a sport-depiction mismatch equivalent to boat-centered XR rowing. It does not compare a physically congruent mode against a visually sport-realistic but kinematically mismatched mode. Therefore, it should not be used as evidence for or against virtual oars, oarlocks, blade motion, or rowing-specific sport realism.

However, it does provide a caution: adding a more immersive display layer can introduce comfort, control, and balance concerns. In a boat-centered XR mode, this means sport-realistic visual immersion should not be assumed to improve experience if it makes the user feel less stable, less in control, or less comfortable.

### Core tradeoff

The paper contributes to the thesis’s core tradeoff by showing that **more immersive display technology is not automatically superior**. The best user experience may depend on a combination of immersion, control, feedback, comfort, familiarity, and user group. This supports the thesis’s broader distinction between different kinds of “realism” or “fidelity.”

For XR rowing, this means the question should not be “is VR more realistic than a screen?” or “is more immersion better?” The better question is: which mapping gives the user the strongest useful combination of embodiment, agency/control, comfort, and sport meaning?

## What not to overclaim

- Do not claim this proves VR rowing works. It tested cycling, not rowing.
- Do not claim this proves XR rowing increases embodiment. It did not measure body ownership, self-location, or embodiment toward an avatar.
- Do not claim HMD VR is worse than screen VR in general. The study found specific engagement advantages for Low Screen VR in this protocol and sample.
- Do not claim Zwift is universally better than HMD VR. The Low Screen condition performed well here, but the result depends on setup, task, user group, and measures.
- Do not claim VR reduces perceived exertion. RPE did not significantly differ across conditions.
- Do not claim VR improves exercise performance. Most performance variables did not show significant condition effects.
- Do not claim older adults cannot use HMD VR. The paper reports issues for some users but not a general impossibility.
- Do not claim the paper proves vection. It did not measure vection directly.
- Do not claim the paper proves presence effects. It discusses presence, but the main engagement measure was Flow State Scale, not a dedicated spatial presence instrument.
- Do not claim high immersion is bad. A more careful wording is that high immersion may introduce comfort, control, and balance tradeoffs.
- Do not claim the Low Room projection condition is equivalent to Zwift or effort-coupled locomotion. The projected room video was not linked to cycling effort.
- Do not use this as evidence for virtual oars, tool embodiment, rowing biomechanics, or erg-vs-boat kinematic mismatch.

## Candidate thesis paragraph

Hibbs et al. (2024) provide a useful caution against treating immersion as a simple design ladder in virtual exercise systems. In their cycling study, participants across young, middle-aged, and older adult groups completed exercise under no-VR, low-screen VR, low-room-projection VR, and HMD VR conditions. While broad performance improvements and reductions in perceived exertion were not observed, the low-screen, effort-responsive condition produced favorable engagement outcomes, especially for sense of control and loss of self-consciousness. For XR rowing, this suggests that HMD-based immersion should not be assumed to be superior to display-based virtual sport systems. Instead, the value of XR should be examined through specific dimensions such as control, comfort, action-outcome congruence, presence, and embodiment, while acknowledging that screen-based Zwift-like systems may remain preferable for many users.

## Candidate short thesis sentence

Hibbs et al. (2024) show that higher display immersion is not automatically superior in virtual exercise, since a low-screen, effort-responsive cycling setup produced favorable engagement outcomes while HMD VR introduced comfort and balance concerns for some users.

## Codex / agent note

Use this paper as the critical counterbalance to more pro-immersion VR exercise papers such as Colombo et al. (2025). It is valuable because it helps the thesis avoid sounding like it assumes XR rowing is inherently better than screen-based rowing apps.

Preserve the distinction between:

- **high immersion HMD VR**,
- **low-screen effort-coupled VR / Zwift-like virtual sport**,
- **low-room projection video**, which was not effort-coupled,
- **no VR**.

The strongest concepts to connect are:

- sense of control,
- flow/engagement,
- loss of self-consciousness,
- transformation of time,
- comfort and cybersickness/balance tradeoffs,
- desktop-first / screen-based virtual sport legitimacy.

Do not use this paper to support body ownership, virtual oar embodiment, or rowing biomechanics. Use it to argue that the thesis is not defending “maximum immersion” as a universal good. The thesis can instead ask which form of congruence and realism matters most for the specific experience being designed.

Pairing recommendation:

- **Colombo et al. 2025** = immersive HMD + interaction can improve enjoyment, attention, presence, preference, and actual effort without higher perceived effort.
- **Hibbs et al. 2024** = low-screen Zwift-like effort-coupled virtual sport may provide stronger control/engagement and broader tolerance than HMD VR.

Together they support a balanced claim: virtual sport benefits are real, but the best format depends on user group, comfort, control, display mode, feedback, and task design.
