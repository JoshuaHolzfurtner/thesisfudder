# Thesis notes — Flood & Simpson, *The Complete Guide to Indoor Rowing* (preview)

## Source identity

Flood, J., & Simpson, C. *The Complete Guide to Indoor Rowing*. Bloomsbury / A&C Black preview excerpt.

The uploaded PDF appears to be a preview containing front matter, the introduction, Chapter 1 (“The development of indoor rowing”), and the beginning of Chapter 2 (“How rowing machines work”).

## How to use this source

Use this mainly as contextual background for the history and practical culture of indoor rowing, not as a core scientific source. It is especially useful for a short “indoor rowing and ergometer context” subsection.

Best uses:
- brief history of rowing machines and indoor rowing
- early patents and old machine designs
- development of ergometers as testing/training devices
- Concept2 standardization and indoor rowing competitions
- adaptive indoor rowing and FES rowing context
- the idea that rowing machines simulate on-water rowing only imperfectly

Avoid using it as the main source for:
- embodiment theory
- biomechanics model details
- rigorous physiology claims
- final form-analysis algorithms
- performance science claims without stronger supporting sources

## Key thesis-relevant points

### Indoor rowing has a long pre-digital history

The book states that rowing machines have been used for about 140 years and identifies W. B. Curtis’s June 1871 U.S. patent as the first rowing-machine patent. It also says more than 40 patents were filed between 1871 and 1952.

This is useful for showing that indoor rowing is not merely a recent digital-fitness trend. The virtual/XR rowing project sits in a long lineage of attempts to reproduce, measure, or train rowing indoors.

### Early rowing machines were simulation and fitness devices

The preview describes early machines as attempts to simulate rowing action and maintain fitness indoors. It notes fixed-seat designs and later sliding-seat development.

Useful thesis angle:
- rowing machines historically translate an on-water movement into an indoor apparatus
- your project adds another translation layer: ergometer action to virtual rowing/boat embodiment

### Ergometer history and measurement culture

The preview connects the rise of evidence-based rowing coaching/testing to the development of machines that could measure force and fitness. It describes an early Australian ergometer developed around Frank Cotton, John Harrison, and Ted Curtain, using “ergon” and “metron” as the basis for the term ergometer.

Useful thesis angle:
- the erg is not only a simulator but also a measuring instrument
- indoor rowing culture is deeply tied to quantification, testing, and standardized comparison

### Concept2 standardization enabled indoor rowing as a sport

The preview emphasizes that adoption of Concept2 as a standard machine enabled fair comparisons across rowers. It describes the Dreissigacker brothers’ development of the Concept2 machines and the Model B display as a major step toward monitoring progress and fair testing.

Useful thesis angle:
- standardization and reliable measurement are central to indoor rowing
- your app can build on this culture by combining physical erg metrics with embodied visual representation

### Indoor rowing competitions and networked racing

The preview mentions CRASH-B, British Indoor Rowing Championships, and internet/virtual competitions. It also notes that Concept2 machines can be connected for competition and racing.

Useful thesis angle:
- virtual or remote competition is already part of indoor rowing’s development path
- XR rowing is a plausible continuation rather than a disconnected novelty

### Machines imperfectly simulate on-water rowing

Chapter 2 explicitly frames rowing machines as attempts to simulate the action/feel of an oar in water, while stating that even the best machines only provide a limited simulation and that the search continues for a machine with the same feel as an oar in water.

This is very relevant for the thesis:
- it supports the claim that erg rowing and boat rowing are mechanically/perceptually different
- it helps justify comparing an erg-congruent representation with a boat-realistic representation
- it prevents naive claims that erg rowing simply “is” rowing in virtual form

### Resistance systems and “feel”

The preview compares resistance mechanisms:
- mechanical brake
- hydraulic resistance
- air impeller
- water rower
- magnetic braking
- floating-head systems

Useful thesis angle:
- different erg designs create different rowing “feel”
- physical interface properties influence the user’s embodied/sport experience
- a virtual rowing system should treat the erg as a real interface/tool with its own mechanics, not as an invisible generic controller

### Floating-head rowing machines

The preview explains that rowing in a boat feels different from a standard rowing machine because the boat and foot stretcher move relative to the rower. Floating-head machines attempt to recreate a more realistic on-water feel.

This is especially relevant:
- it directly supports your distinction between erg-centered and boat-centered representation
- it shows that the machine industry already recognizes the mismatch between static ergs and on-water dynamics

## Suggested thesis placement

Use in a short background paragraph like:

“Indoor rowing machines have long attempted to reproduce rowing action indoors, first as training and fitness devices and later as standardized competition machines. However, even dedicated rowing-machine literature emphasizes that rowing machines provide only an approximation of the feel of an oar in water. This historical and mechanical distinction motivates the present comparison between an ergometer-congruent representation and a more boat-realistic virtual rowing depiction.”
