# Thesis notes — Niehorster 2021, “Optic Flow: A History”

## Citation identity

Niehorster, D. C. (2021). *Optic Flow: A History*. *i-Perception*, 12(6), 1-49. https://doi.org/10.1177/20416695211055766

## What this paper is

This is a historical and conceptual article about the development of the concept of optic flow. It is not a VR paper, not an embodiment paper, and not a rowing study. The paper reconstructs how the idea of optic flow developed through James J. Gibson, Gwilym C. Grindley, Edward S. Calvert, J. T. MacCurdy, and several earlier or parallel sources. It uses scientific literature and archival research to discuss who described which parts of the concept, how these authors may have influenced each other, and why the emergence of optic flow is better understood as convergent scientific development than as a single simple invention story.

For thesis use, the most important part is the paper's clear framing of optic flow as a global pattern of visual motion caused by and signalling self-motion. The introduction describes forward walking as a case where trees, shrubs, and other environmental elements appear to move outward from an optically static point in the direction of travel. More generally, when an observer translates or rotates through the world, the light pattern at the point of observation changes in a lawful continuous perspective transformation. These global motion patterns are called optic flow.

The paper also notes that optic flow is useful for behaving observers: it can support perception of self-motion direction, steering, balance and posture control, collision avoidance, and perception of object shape and environmental layout. That makes the paper useful for defining why moving scenery in virtual sport is not merely decorative, but a perceptually meaningful visual-motion structure.

## Why it matters for the XR rowing thesis

My thesis compares:

1. **Erg-centered / high-congruence XR rowing**
   - real erg handle, seat, and body motion are mapped closely to the virtual avatar
   - prioritizes visuomotor, spatial, temporal, haptic/proprioceptive, and bodily/device congruence

2. **Boat-centered / sport-depiction XR rowing**
   - the user still rows on an erg
   - the virtual avatar appears to row in a boat with oars, oarlocks, blades, feathering/squaring, and boat movement
   - prioritizes rowing-specific sport-action realism
   - introduces mismatch because the virtual oar handles and blades do not move exactly like the real erg handle

The central question is whether embodiment in XR rowing benefits more from precise movement correspondence or from a more realistic sport depiction.

Niehorster's paper does not answer that question directly. It does, however, support one important background mechanism: **virtual movement through a world can be described in terms of simulated optic flow**. In real rowing, the athlete is actually displaced through water, and the water, shoreline, buoys, and other boats create real optic flow. In XR rowing or Zwift-like systems, the user is physically stationary, but the system can recreate optic-flow-like visual motion by moving the virtual boat, avatar, or camera through an environment.

This is important for the thesis because the rowing prototype does not only show an avatar. It also turns stationary ergometer effort into virtual locomotion. The user rows in place, but the visual world changes as if the rower is moving through a river or route. That simulated optic flow may support spatial presence, vection-like self-motion impressions, perceived action consequence, and the feeling that the rowing effort has become meaningful progress rather than static repetition.

The paper therefore belongs in the theoretical background or glossary near terms such as optic flow, vection, virtual locomotion, spatial presence, action-outcome congruence, and the working Anti-Sisyphus framing.

## Core findings / cheat sheet

- **Optic flow is a global visual-motion pattern associated with self-motion.**
  - What was found / argued: the paper defines optic flow as a lawful pattern of visual motion that occurs when an observer moves or rotates relative to the environment.
  - Why it matters: this gives a stable basis for describing the visual movement of water, shoreline, trees, buoys, and route scenery in XR rowing.
  - Strength for thesis: strong for definition/background; not direct evidence for embodiment.

- **Forward movement creates a characteristic expanding pattern.**
  - What was found / argued: during forward movement, objects in the visual image move outward from a relatively stable point in the direction of travel.
  - Why it matters: this is highly intuitive for explaining why virtual forward movement can visually feel like travelling through a world.
  - Strength for thesis: strong for explaining optic flow in accessible language.

- **Optic flow can support perception and guidance of self-motion.**
  - What was found / argued: the paper connects optic flow to perceived self-motion direction, steering, balance/posture control, collision avoidance, and perceived environmental layout.
  - Why it matters: this helps justify optic flow as a meaningful perceptual cue in virtual sport systems, not simply as moving background art.
  - Strength for thesis: medium-strong for theoretical background; still needs VR/vection/cybersickness papers for headset-specific claims.

- **The history of optic flow is not only Gibson.**
  - What was found / argued: Gibson is the canonical figure, but the paper details related and partly independent contributions by Grindley, Calvert, MacCurdy, and others.
  - Why it matters: this is useful if the thesis wants a careful historical footnote or wants to avoid a simplistic “Gibson invented optic flow” claim.
  - Strength for thesis: low-medium unless writing a glossary/history paragraph.

- **Grindley and Calvert discussed aircraft landing and visual guidance.**
  - What was found / argued: several early discussions of optic-flow-like patterns appeared in the context of aircraft landing and visual control.
  - Why it matters: this gives an action-guidance lineage: optic flow is relevant to controlling movement, not only passively perceiving scenery.
  - Strength for thesis: medium for theoretical framing, but the aircraft context should not be overextended to rowing without caution.

## Relation to thesis concepts

### Optic flow

High relevance. This is the main reason to include the paper. The paper supports a strict definition of optic flow as a global pattern of visual motion caused by and signalling self-motion. In the thesis, this should be used to describe the visual-motion structure of moving through a virtual rowing environment.

Useful glossary phrasing:

> Optic flow is the visual motion pattern that normally appears when an observer moves through an environment. In XR rowing, the user remains physically stationary, but the system simulates a similar visual-motion structure by moving the virtual boat, avatar, or camera through the environment in response to rowing effort.

### Vection

Indirect relevance. The paper is about optic flow, not primarily about vection. It can support the visual-motion side of the explanation, but it should not be used alone to claim that users felt self-motion. Pair with a vection or VR locomotion source for the subjective sensation of self-motion.

Best distinction:

- optic flow = the visual motion pattern
- vection = the felt impression of self-motion that optic flow may help create

### Presence / Place Illusion

Indirect relevance. Optic flow may contribute to the feeling of being in a moving virtual environment, but the paper is not a presence paper. Use it to support the perceptual basis of virtual movement, not the full claim of presence.

### Plausibility Illusion

Indirect relevance. In XR rowing, believable world movement can support the plausibility of the rowing scene. However, Niehorster 2021 does not test Plausibility Illusion. Use it only as background for the visual-motion cue, not as evidence that the scenario feels real.

### Action-outcome congruence

Moderate conceptual relevance. The paper discusses optic flow as a visual structure associated with self-motion and action guidance. For the thesis, this connects to the idea that a stroke should produce visible movement through the world. However, the paper does not study user action mapped to virtual feedback, so the thesis should use more direct agency/action-outcome sources for that claim.

### Sense of agency

Indirect relevance. Optic flow can become part of the feedback that makes the user feel their rowing action caused virtual movement. But Niehorster 2021 does not measure agency. Do not cite it as agency evidence. Use it only to define the visual consequence that may participate in agency.

### Body ownership

Low relevance. The paper does not test virtual body ownership, Rubber Hand Illusion, or avatar embodiment. It should not be used to support body ownership claims.

### Self-location

Indirect relevance. Optic flow is relevant to perceived self-motion and movement through a world, but this paper should not be used as the main source for self-location in embodiment. Use it as background for visual motion and pair it with embodiment/self-location sources.

### Spatial alignment

Low to indirect relevance. The paper is not about aligning real and virtual handles, seats, bodies, or machines. It can support the larger visual movement context, but not the specific alignment argument.

### Temporal congruence / latency

Low to indirect relevance. The paper does not test latency between action and feedback. For XR rowing, temporal congruence is relevant because optic flow must update in time with the rowing stroke, but that claim needs other sources.

### Visuomotor congruence

Indirect relevance. Optic flow can be part of visuomotor coupling when movement is driven by the user’s action. But the paper is not specifically about matching seen limb movement to performed movement.

### VR motion sickness / cybersickness

Indirect relevance only. Optic flow is relevant to VR motion, but this paper should not be used as evidence about cybersickness or comfort. It does not test VR sickness, stationary exercise, or rowing. Pair with cybersickness / simulator sickness / vection sources.

### Rowing biomechanics

No direct relevance. The paper does not discuss rowing movement, ergometers, oar kinematics, stroke phases, or water biomechanics.

### Erg vs on-water rowing

No direct relevance. The paper can support the optic-flow difference between stationary erg use and virtual/on-water travel, but not the biomechanics of erg-vs-boat rowing.

## How it maps to my thesis modes

### Erg-centered mode

The erg-centered mode prioritizes accurate mapping between the user’s real handle/seat/body motion and the avatar. Niehorster 2021 does not directly support that embodiment claim. However, it can still support the surrounding locomotion layer: if the erg-centered avatar produces virtual forward movement through the environment, the resulting visual motion can be described as simulated optic flow.

In this mode, the key thesis claim should be:

> The erg-centered mode may combine high bodily/device congruence with effort-coupled optic flow: the user’s real rowing action closely controls the avatar and also produces virtual route progression.

That is a stronger and more specific claim than simply saying “it is more realistic.”

### Boat-centered mode

The boat-centered mode prioritizes sport-action realism: virtual oars, oarlocks, blades, boat movement, and the expected visual logic of on-water rowing. Niehorster 2021 supports the locomotion/world-motion side of this representation, because on-water rowing naturally includes optic flow from the moving boat. The virtual river scene can recreate this visual-motion structure while the user remains on an erg.

In this mode, the key thesis claim should be:

> The boat-centered mode may strengthen sport-action congruence by restoring the visual logic of rowing through a world: the boat advances, water and shoreline move past, and the user receives optic-flow-like cues that are absent in ordinary stationary ergometer use.

However, the paper does not justify the oar/handle mismatch. That tradeoff needs to be argued through embodiment, agency, and congruence literature.

### Core tradeoff

Niehorster 2021 contributes mostly to the **sport-action / virtual locomotion** side of the tradeoff, not to the core body-ownership evidence.

The paper helps define one reason boat-centered XR rowing may feel more like rowing as a sport: it can recreate the visual movement through a rowing environment. But it does not tell us whether that sport-action realism is more important than the precise visuomotor match between real handle movement and avatar movement.

Core thesis wording:

> Niehorster 2021 supports the optic-flow part of the rowing simulation: XR rowing can visually recreate the motion structure normally produced by travelling through a rowing environment. It does not determine whether this visual locomotion should be prioritized over close handle/body congruence.

## What not to overclaim

- Do not claim this paper proves XR rowing embodiment.
- Do not claim this paper tested VR, headsets, avatars, or rowing machines.
- Do not claim this paper proves Zwift, Holofit, EXR, or XR rowing creates real self-motion.
- Do not claim optic flow automatically produces vection.
- Do not claim optic flow automatically produces presence, Place Illusion, Plausibility Illusion, agency, or body ownership.
- Do not claim optic flow solves cybersickness or prevents VR motion sickness.
- Do not claim this paper validates the Anti-Sisyphus effect as a scientific construct.
- Do not claim this paper proves that rowing effort coupled to visual movement is comfortable or nausea-free.
- Do not claim this paper proves that virtual oars, rowing blades, or boat movement increase embodiment.
- Do not claim this paper supports erg-vs-on-water biomechanics.
- Do not use this paper as the main evidence for temporal congruence, latency, or action-outcome agency.

## Candidate thesis paragraph

Optic flow describes the global pattern of visual motion that occurs when an observer moves through an environment. During real locomotion, nearby surfaces, objects, and environmental structure move across the visual field in a lawful way, providing information about self-motion and the surrounding layout. In XR rowing, the user remains physically stationary on an ergometer, but the system can simulate a similar visual-motion structure by moving the virtual boat or camera through a rowing environment in response to the user's stroke. This makes optic flow relevant for understanding how stationary rowing effort can be transformed into apparent virtual locomotion. However, optic flow should be distinguished from vection and embodiment: it is the visual motion cue itself, not necessarily the subjective feeling of self-motion or ownership over the avatar.

## Candidate short thesis sentence

In XR rowing, effort-coupled optic flow can make stationary ergometer work visually appear as locomotion through a rowing environment, but this visual cue should not be confused with full embodiment or body ownership.

## Codex / agent note

Use this paper mainly to support the glossary and theory section for **optic flow**. Preserve the distinction between optic flow and vection. Optic flow is the visual motion pattern; vection is the possible subjective feeling of self-motion. For Zwift-like systems, write “simulated optic flow” or “optic-flow-like visual motion,” because the user is physically stationary and the world motion is generated by the display. For VR/XR rowing, the term is still useful, especially because the visual field can be immersive and the movement is coupled to rowing effort.

Do not use this paper as direct evidence for embodiment, agency, ownership, avatar realism, rowing biomechanics, or cybersickness. It is a theoretical/background support paper. Best placement: glossary, related work on visual self-motion, and design rationale for why stroke-coupled virtual locomotion matters.
