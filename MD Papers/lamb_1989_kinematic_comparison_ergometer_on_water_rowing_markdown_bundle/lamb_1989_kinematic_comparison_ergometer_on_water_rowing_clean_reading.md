A kinematic comparison of ergometer and
on-water rowing
DOUGLAS H. LAMB,* PhD
From the Department of Physical Education, Biomechanics Laboratory, and Lambert
Gymnasium, Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana
ABSTRACT
The purpose of this investigation was to compare onwater rowing with ergometer rowing by identifying the
important kinematic variables, using vector loop analysis. Two hypotheses were investigated that provided
evidence that rowing ergometers are valid simulators
of actual rowing and that the trunk segment is the
major contributor to the total linear oar velocity. Thirty
subjects were filmed rowing on-water and on an ergometer. The film was digitized for X and Y coordinates of
the body joint locations for a cycle of rowing from catch
to finish. A model of rowing was designed such that
the rowing movement could be defined as comprising
two closed vector loops. The digitized data were fitted
to the vector loop model to derive kinematic variables
from the drive phase of rowing. Five kinematic variables
that described the contributions of five body segments
to the total linear oar velocity were analyzed statistically
to find differences in the patterns of on-water and
ergometer rowing. The results indicated that the kinematics of the upper arm and forearm segments were
significantly different in the two types of rowing. These
differences were of minor importance because of the
small contributions made by the arms at the catch. The
differences at the finish were a result of the lifting of
the oar from the water not exhibited in ergometer
rowing. The vector loop analysis provided the major
finding that the trunk segment contributed significantly
more to the drive phase portion of the rowing cycle for
both on-water and ergometer rowing although the legs
initiated the drive phase. Despite the differences that
occurred in the arm motion at the beginning and end of
the drive phase, most of the kinematic variables were
similar throughout the drive phase, particularly for the
legs and trunk. The objective of developing a biomechanical test that uses kinematic measures obtained
on an ergometer is supported by the findings of this
study, and such a study would supplement physiologic
measures used in the selection of rowers.
Rowing is a sport that demands a high technical skill level
as well as strength and other physiologic requirements.
Training programs are often designed to include on-land
conditioning with rowing simulators that approximate the
rowing motion. The popularity of these training devices,
which exist in several designs, is such that there are now
&dquo;indoor rowing olympics&dquo; held on computerized rowing simulators.’ These machines have an advantage over on-water
rowing in that they are stationary, they do not involve the
elements of drag from water, and they can measure work
expenditure to convey an instant feedback to the athlete.
Although off-water rowing simulators, also known as rowing ergometers, have been used extensively for the physiologic testing of rowers,6,7 little evidence in the literature
exists to support the belief that ergometer rowing replicates
the same patterns of motion as used in on-water rowing.7,9,10
The United States Rowing Association uses as part of their
selection process of candidates for the national rowing
teams, physiologic measurements obtained from rowing ergometer tests. At present, there is no biomechanical test
designed to evaluate rowing kinematics. A rowing ergometer
capable of simulating the biomechanics of on-water rowing
would be a valuable tool for use in team selection, training,
and technical coaching of rowers.
The rowing stroke consists of a cyclic sequence of events
that includes the catch, the drive phase, the finish, and the
recovery. The catch occurs as the oar is placed quickly in
the water and is followed by the drive phase, which includes
the movement of the oar through the water.4,8,12 The finish
occurs as the oar is withdrawn from the water and is followed
by the recovery as the oar is moved through the air and is
prepared to reenter the water. This study deals only with
the analysis of the drive phase, because it is the drive phase
that best allows an understanding of how the rower’s shell

The literature is inconclusive concerning an accepted
sequence of body actions for initiating the drive phase in
the rowing stroke. Edwards’ described the trunk-thigh angle
relationship as one of knee extension as well as trunk
extension. Contraction of the hamstrings causes backward
rotation of the trunk about the hips and increases the thightrunk angle. However, it is possible to increase the thightrunk angle by fixing the trunk and lowering the thighs by
extension of the knees. If the quadriceps alone extend the
knee, this extension will merely push the slide away to the
backstop. If the knee is not extended, contraction of the
hamstrings will cause the trunk to pivot about the hip joint.
For proper opening of the trunk-thigh angle, the quadriceps
and hamstrings should work simultaneously, with the quadriceps extending the knee and the hamstrings rotating the
trunk. Improper use of the hamstrings results in &dquo;bumshoving&dquo; or &dquo;shooting the tail&dquo; (the action of knee extension
without the oar actually being pulled through the water).
Williamsl2 determined that the speed of the blade relative
to the shell during the drive phase is closely related to the
speed of the shell through the water. Williams believed that
the drive is initiated by the motion of the trunk and suggested that the proximal joint must be stabilized before
distal joints are moved so that the sequence of the drive
phase is an extension developing from the hips. The thightrunk angle must be the first to be opened, which explains
why the initial drive must come from the trunk and not the
legs.
Rosenberg (United States National Team Rowing Coach,
1975 to 1976) argued (unpublished data&dquo;) that if the stroke
is performed properly, the legs accelerate the shell when it
is at its lowest velocity and that then the lower trunk,
midtrunk, arm, and wrist muscles become involved as the
velocity of the shell increases. Martin and Bernfields argued
that the legs initiate the drive and are the prime source of
power. Nelson and Widulel° obtained results confirming that
because of knee extension, the legs were the primary forceproducing segments of the body in the early stage of the
drive phase.
Vector loop modeling has seen limited application to the
study of human movements 11 It has been frequently used
for the study of mechanism analysis. Hall’ used its principles
to develop the concept of kinematic coefficients where the
motion of a mechanism can be expressed in terms of reference positions. The term &dquo;kinematic coefficient&dquo; represents
the derivative of a motion variable with respect to an input
or reference variable and allows for an understanding of how
motion occurs within the framework of a body or machine.
There are two important purposes to this paper. The first
purpose was to compare the on-water kinematics of the drive
phase of the rowing cycle to the kinematics obtained on a
dry land simulator, the rowing ergometer. There is little
indication in the literature of this having been previously
attempted. It was hoped that by testing the hypothesis that
ergometer rowing simulates on-water rowing, the results
would support a proposed biomechanical test composed of
kinematic variables to train and select future rowers. A
second purpose of the study was to develop a vector loop
model of rowing that would provide the framework for a
comparison of on-water and ergometer rowing kinematics
in order to determine the correct sequence of actions in the
drive phase of sweep oar rowing.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
The subjects were 30 male candidates invited to the U.S.
National Heavyweight Rowing Team selection camp of 1982.
A Stanford Rowing Ergometer (Robert Austin Co.,) designed
to simulate the technique and feel of on-water sweep rowing
in an eight-oared shell, was used for the study. All of the
subjects were filmed rowing on-water and on the rowing
ergometer while they rowed at 30 to 31 strokes per minute
and were instructed to use the same technique throughout
the study.
All filming (ergometer and on-water) was done using a
Milliken, Model DBM55 (D.B. Milliken Co., Arcadia, CA),
16 mm camera with a 50 mm lens and a 72° shutter with a
film speed of 50 frames/sec that was verified by an internal
pulse generator set at 10 pulses per second. For the ergometer filming, the camera was positioned at right angles to
the plane of motion at a distance of 15 m. A board marked
with a distance of 1 m was filmed in the location of the
rower’s body to determine the conversion factor from film
length to actual linear length.
The on-water filming session involved mounting the camera on a motor-propelled launch that traveled at a comparable speed to the moving crew of eight rowers. The distance
between the launch and the moving crew was approximately
20 m. The camera was focused on only one rower at a time,
and the film was exposed for approximately six consecutive
strokes of each rower.
From the processed film, every frame of the drive phase
of one stroke was examined; sample sizes ranged from 40 to
62 frames. For each frame of film, nine points were plotted
(toe, ankle, knee, hip, shoulder, elbow, wrist, oar handle, and
head). The location of the seat was assumed to be represented by the position of the hip joint. The X and Y
coordinates of these locations were assigned by the use of a
Numonics digitizer (Numonics Corp., Lansdale, PA) linked
with the Purdue Remote Control On-Line System
(PROCSY) at the Biomechanics Laboratory, Lambert Gymnasium, Purdue University, and were subsequently stored
as computer data files for each subject.
The data file coordinates were corrected for the inherent
noise (error) by digital filtering to smooth the displacement
data before the computation of kinematic information. Selection of filter coefficients corresponding to the fourth
harmonic level (cut-off frequency) were used in a secondorder Butterworth recursive digital filter to model the raw
data.16 It was determined that this filter resulted in accept-

between subjects. The data of filtered coordinates, angular
displacements, and angular velocities were then fitted to a
vector loop model of a rower (Fig. 1).
The vector loop model allows for a kinematic analysis to
study the state of motion (position, velocity, and acceleration) of each body segment as represented by linkages within
a vector loop. In this study, the problem was to decide which
kinematic variables were of primary interest to compare
ergometer rowing with on-water rowing. A random selection
of kinematic variables would likely cause omission of some
of the important variables or cause inclusion of too many
variables to satisfy statistical criteria. The vector loop model
eliminates this problem and, through a process of solving
closed position and velocity vector equations, the kinematics
of each body segment are directly related to the relative
velocity of the hand, seat, and shell. To use the vector loop
model of a rower (Fig. 1) in this study, it was assumed that
the motion of the body segments in rowing is two-dimensional. This assumption was deemed valid by several rowing
authorities and crew members at Purdue University. The
tendency of the elbows to move away from the sagittal plane
was assumed to be of minor consequence and did not present

To compare the obtained kinematic variables for the two
types of rowing, the statistical design used was a repeated
measures, two factors, analysis of variance (ANOVA) with
the subjects nested within the factors. The two factors were
the type of rowing and the percent of drive phase.
The factor of percent of drive phase compared each dependent kinematic variable at each 10% of the drive phase
for nine successive intervals, excluding the end points of 0%
and 100%. The percent of drive phase was determined by

(VELVAR4) means for on-water rowing and ergometer rowing.
dividing the frame number by the total number of frames
and multiplying by 100. Each 10% level value was obtained
by selecting the measure closest to 10% or a multiple of
10%. Therefore, kinematic measures were obtained at 10%,
20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90% of the drive
phase.
The ANOVA tested for the presence of interaction between the type of rowing and the percent of the drive phase.
A post hoc Scheffe test was used for determining which pairs
of group means were statistically different when the kinematic variables had a positive interaction.

DISCUSSION
The vector loop model of rowing helped to identify the
contributions made by the body segments to the total linear
oar velocity. The subjects of this study exhibited similar
kinematics between on-water and ergometer rowing for both
the trunk and leg components. They also demonstrated that
these components are the prime contributors to the drive
phase of sweep oar rowing. It is apparent from comparing
Figure 5 with Figures 2 and 6, that the lower leg is responsible for initiating the drive phase in sweep oar rowing.
Somewhere between 40% and 50% of the drive phase, the
trunk contribution equals and then surpasses the contribution made by the lower legs.
The statistical differences between the two types of rowing
for the upper and lower arm components occurred only at
the beginning and end of the drive phase (Figs. 3 and 4).
The differences at the beginning of the drive phase can be
explained by realizing that slippage of the oar in the water
creates a situation in which the arms compensate for the
nonstationary fulcrum of the oar in the water. The catch

technological changes in design were to be made to the

The arm segment motions (VELVAR2 and VELVAR3)
were also significantly different at the end of the drive phase
(P < 
0.05). This may have been the result of a reduced
tendency to &dquo;feather&dquo; or lift the oar at the finish in ergometer
rowing, which is essential in on-water rowing. For practical
purposes, ergometer rowing should emphasize the motions
of feathering the oar or hyperextending the wrists at the
finish. In this study, the results indicated that the subjects
did not decelerate the lower arm movements at the end of
the drive phase for ergometer rowing and generated a greater
relative hand velocity at the finish.
The fact that significant differences did occur between
the two types of rowing for the arm segments should not be
interpreted as grounds for rejecting the original hypothesis
because the absolute measures of the arm component velocities were relatively small in comparison to the contributions
made by the trunk and legs at the beginning of the drive
phase. The significant differences obtained for the arms
would appear to be of minor importance when consideration
is given to the contributions made by the different body
segments to the total linear velocity.
In conclusion, the vector loop analysis was able to generate
important kinematic information for the motions of rowing
and proved that the trunk segment of the body is the major
contributor to generating total linear oar velocity. This is in
contrast to the research of Martin and Bernfield,s who stated
that the legs are the prime source of power.
The hypothesis that ergometer rowing simulates on-water
rowing was supported from the findings of this study. Despite some differences that occurred in arm motion at the
beginning and end of the drive phase, the kinematic variables of the legs and trunk were similar between on-water
and ergometer rowing. The objective of developing a biomechanical test that uses kinematic measures obtained on
an ergometer would appear to be valid in supplementing
physiological measures used in the selection of rowers.
